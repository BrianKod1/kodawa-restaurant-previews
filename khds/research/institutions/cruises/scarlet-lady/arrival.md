# Arrival

## Research question

How are terminal arrival, luggage, check-in, boarding, accessibility assistance and the transition into Scarlet Lady's onboard systems coordinated?

## Evidence used

Primary evidence:

- S2 — Virgin Voyages Port Information FAQ
- S6 — Virgin Voyages Using The Band FAQ
- S10 — Virgin Voyages Accessibility FAQ
- S12 — Virgin Voyages Accessibility page

Supporting evidence:

- S3 — My Account FAQ
- S5 — Sailor App FAQ

This file describes the published arrival sequence. It does not establish queue times, successful boarding, credential activation, luggage delivery or assistance performance.

## Arrival is a controlled transition

Scarlet Lady's arrival process is not represented as unrestricted entry.

Virgin publishes a sequence in which the Sailor:

1. completes Ready to Sail before the voyage;
2. selects a Terminal Arrival Time;
3. prepares travel and luggage documentation;
4. arrives according to the selected slot;
5. completes terminal check-in;
6. hands off luggage where applicable;
7. crosses the terminal/gangway boundary;
8. enters the ship;
9. begins using The Band and onboard systems.

That sequence makes arrival a transfer between several operational owners rather than one doorway event.

## Terminal Arrival Time

Virgin states that general boarding usually begins at 2:15 p.m.

However, Sailors are told to follow their selected Terminal Arrival Time to complete terminal check-in.

These are different concepts:

**general boarding start**

is not the same as

**individual authorized arrival time.**

Virgin also states that terminal locations can change and advises Sailors to check voyage details before sailing.

The operational implication is that both time and place remain voyage-specific.

## Early arrival

Some home-port guidance explicitly discourages treating early arrival as early entry.

For Terminal V in Miami, Virgin states that luggage may be accepted from 9:00 a.m., but Sailors should return no earlier than 15 minutes before their chosen arrival time.

Virgin also states that arriving early does not mean the Sailor will be admitted early.

This creates a useful KHDS distinction:

**luggage acceptance**

can occur before

**guest terminal admission.**

The two should not be treated as the same service.

## Luggage preparation

Virgin states that Sailors receive an email seven days before embarkation containing printable luggage tags after completing Ready to Sail steps.

The tags are intended to include identifying information that helps route luggage to the correct cabin.

If the email does not arrive, or the Sailor forgets to print the tags, Virgin states that luggage tags are available at the terminal.

That provides a published fallback route.

The fallback does not prove that baggage will be delivered correctly or on time.

No baggage-delivery performance has been observed.

## Port-specific variation

Arrival procedures are not fully uniform across every home port.

The Port Information FAQ contains port-specific instructions for locations including Miami, San Juan, Seattle, Vancouver, Barcelona and others.

Examples include different:

- terminal addresses;
- luggage-drop arrangements;
- waiting-area conditions;
- departure times;
- parking;
- transfer options.

Case #26 should therefore avoid treating one home-port procedure as universal Scarlet Lady policy.

The institutional experience is mobile, but the arrival environment is partly determined by the land-side port.

## Check-in

Virgin directs Sailors to complete Ready to Sail in the app before arrival.

Terminal check-in then remains a separate physical transition.

The public evidence does not establish:

- average processing time;
- identity-verification failure recovery;
- passport/document exception handling;
- what happens after missing a Terminal Arrival Time;
- whether all app failures can be resolved at the terminal.

Those remain open.

## Accessibility handoff

Virgin states that wheelchair assistance is available at home ports on embarkation and disembarkation days.

The published assistance boundary is:

terminal entrance
→ entrance of the gangway.

Virgin explicitly distinguishes this from ordinary port-call assistance.

This creates a responsibility boundary between:

- land-side terminal assistance;
- gangway transition;
- onboard accessibility support.

The public evidence explains the boundary but does not establish waiting times, staffing capacity or continuity across it.

## Entering the ship

Once onboard, the guest enters a different operating environment.

The Band is published as a voyage-specific wearable credential used for:

- cabin access;
- touchless payments;
- identity confirmation through the encrypted ID associated with the Sailor's security photo.

S6 states that each Band is associated with one specific voyage.

This makes The Band an important arrival artifact because it represents the transition from terminal identity to onboard identity.

## Identity continuity

A central Case #26 issue emerges at this point.

Before arrival, the Sailor exists in:

- booking records;
- account systems;
- Sailor App;
- Ready to Sail information;
- accessibility or medical requests.

After arrival, the same person must also be recognized through:

- terminal systems;
- security records;
- The Band;
- cabin access;
- onboard account;
- onboard service teams.

The system therefore depends on continuity across multiple representations of the same guest.

No end-to-end identity synchronization test has been performed.

## Band loss and identity control

Virgin states that a lost Band cannot simply be used by another Sailor because the encrypted ID allows Crew devices to display the associated security photo.

Crew are expected to verify that the person wearing the Band matches the stored photo.

That describes a security control.

It does not establish:

- Band replacement time;
- temporary cabin-access alternatives;
- how charges are blocked after loss;
- how quickly a new Band propagates across onboard systems.

Those are important recovery questions for the stay dimension.

## What public evidence establishes

Current evidence supports these bounded findings:

1. Arrival is governed by an individual Terminal Arrival Time.
2. General boarding time is not unrestricted guest access.
3. Some ports permit luggage drop before terminal admission.
4. Printable luggage tags are issued after Ready to Sail completion.
5. Terminal-issued luggage tags provide a published fallback.
6. Arrival procedures vary by home port.
7. Home-port wheelchair assistance has a defined terminal-to-gangway boundary.
8. The Band becomes a voyage-specific onboard identity, access and payment credential.

## What remains unresolved

The current evidence does not establish:

- queue durations;
- missed-slot recovery;
- terminal congestion;
- failed-document handling;
- luggage delivery time;
- lost luggage recovery;
- accessibility-assistance wait times;
- Band issuance or replacement performance;
- cabin readiness timing;
- synchronization between terminal and onboard records;
- first-use friction in the Sailor App or Band.

## KHDS interpretation

Arrival on Scarlet Lady is best understood as a chain of handoffs:

pre-arrival preparation
→ port arrival
→ luggage handoff
→ terminal identity check
→ accessibility handoff where needed
→ gangway
→ onboard identity
→ cabin/service access

Each transition changes either the responsible team, the physical environment, or the guest's active credential.

This makes arrival a useful test of **identity continuity across operational boundaries**.

A guest can complete each individual step successfully while still experiencing failure if information, luggage, accessibility requirements or credential state do not transfer correctly between steps.

## Evidence rating

**Partial.**

The published process is detailed enough to describe the intended arrival architecture.

Operational execution, exception handling and continuity across handoffs remain unverified.
