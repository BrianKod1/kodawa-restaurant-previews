1. Purpose of this file

This file studies departure as the final operational sequence of a stay at Fogo Island Inn.

It does not reduce departure to:

checkout time,

key return,

payment,

luggage collection,

or a farewell at the door.

Fogo Island is reached through a journey involving multiple systems.

For many guests, departure can involve:

final-night preparation,

closing the room,

reviewing the account,

returning borrowed equipment,

transferring luggage,

receiving a departure lunch pack,

travelling to the ferry terminal,

crossing by ferry,

continuing by vehicle,

and reaching an airport, rental-car return or other independently usable travel state.

Some of those stages belong to the Inn.

Some belong to external operators.

Some depend on the route the guest selected.

The research problem is therefore not merely whether checkout is efficient.

It is whether the institution closes its responsibilities without leaving the guest to reconstruct a complex onward journey at the point of greatest consequence.

2. Departure boundary

Departure begins before the guest leaves the room.

It may begin when:

the final itinerary is confirmed,

a ferry crossing is selected,

transport is reconfirmed,

luggage collection is scheduled,

the account becomes reviewable,

breakfast or a lunch pack is arranged,

or disruption becomes likely.

It does not necessarily end at the Inn's front door.

The end point depends on what the institution agreed to coordinate.

Checkout
≠
Departure

Checkout is a room-and-account event.

Departure is a responsibility-transfer sequence.

2.1 Physical boundary

The guest may physically leave the Inn while still relying on:

Inn-arranged transport,

Inn-coordinated ferry timing,

luggage under another party's custody,

or an itinerary constructed by Guest Itinerary Advisors.

Physical exit therefore cannot be assumed to equal service completion.

2.2 Digital boundary

Departure also includes information that may need to remain usable after leaving the building:

itinerary,

driver or vehicle identity,

ferry status,

transfer timing,

receipt,

support contact,

disruption instructions,

and baggage status.

2.3 Relationship boundary

Post-stay communication may continue the relationship.

It must not be confused with the operational duty to complete the departure safely.

Operational Closure
→
Guest Consent
→
Optional Relationship Continuation

3. Primary research question

How should a remote island hotel close the room, account and stay while continuing responsibility far enough to place the guest into the next independently usable stage of travel?

6. Verified public facts

6.1 Departure-related inclusions

The Inn publicly includes:

a departure lunch pack,

valet service,

departure ferry coordination within valet service,

on-island shuttle service,

meals and non-alcoholic beverages,

and carbon offsetting that includes ground transportation.

6.2 Additional-cost boundary

Transport to and from Fogo Island is listed as an additional-cost enhancement.

Alcoholic beverages and some seasonal entrepreneur-led excursions are also additional.

6.3 Route structure

The public journey from Gander normally includes:

road travel to Farewell ferry terminal,

a ferry crossing to Fogo Island,

and an on-island transfer to or from the Inn.

The Getting Here page describes:

a one-hour drive from Gander to Farewell,

a ferry crossing of approximately 45 minutes,

a round-trip chauffeured shuttle from and to Gander,

self-drive rental-car travel,

and charter aircraft or helicopter access.

6.4 Chauffeured transport

The published round-trip chauffeured shuttle price is CAD $1,150 for one to four people.

This is a current public price, not a permanent KHDS rule.

6.5 Advisor support

Guest Itinerary Advisors publicly offer help with:

routing,

ground transfers,

private aircraft or helicopter charter,

and timing.

6.6 Booking payment state

The reservation terms state that:

a non-refundable 50% deposit confirms the reservation,

the remaining accommodation balance is due 30 days before arrival,

and 15% harmonized sales tax applies.

This suggests that the final account may concentrate on:

additional transport,

alcohol,

paid excursions,

retail,

changes,

or other exceptions.

It does not prove the form or timing of the final bill.

6.7 Access needs

The FAQ asks guests with mobility requirements to contact the Advisors team.

Children aged eight and older are ordinarily welcome.

Service animals and emotional-support animals may be accepted with documentation.

These facts affect departure planning as well as arrival.

14. Checkout function

Checkout has at least four jobs:

close room entitlement,

close or clarify the account,

preserve unresolved issues,

and prepare the next handoff.

A fast checkout that loses an unresolved issue is not successful.

A warm farewell that leaves the guest uncertain about transport is not complete.

21. Early breakfast and Daybreak

23. Ferry coordination

Ferry coordination is consequence-bearing because the ferry is an external service.

A complete coordination model would answer:

Which crossing is targeted?

Is that crossing scheduled, queued or reserved?

When should the guest leave the Inn?

Who checks live service status?

What happens if the route changes?

Who informs the guest?

What happens to onward road or flight bookings?

The website proves that coordination is included.

It does not publish these mechanics.

24. Ferry variability

The provincial ferry system is outside the Inn's direct control.

The public schedule distinguishes routes and crossings.

A direct crossing may take approximately 45 minutes.

A routing that also serves Change Islands may take longer.

Operationally, this means:

the scheduled departure time is not the whole travel time,

route type matters,

loading and queue time matter,

and downstream connections require buffers.

The Inn should translate the external schedule into guest consequences.

This is Ferry-to-Flight Reconciliation.

A recovery system should define:

33. Missed ferry

If the guest misses the intended ferry, the system should answer:

Is another crossing available?

Does the transport remain available?

Where does the guest wait?

What happens to onward travel?

Who communicates the new plan?

Who bears additional cost?

The answer may differ according to cause.

That cause should inform recovery without becoming a reason to abandon the guest.

34. Missed flight

A missed flight can propagate beyond the Inn's formal service boundary.

If Inn-arranged transport or ferry advice contributed, the institution should preserve:

itinerary history,

event times,

operator information,

support ownership,

and communication records.

If the guest independently chose an insufficient connection, the Inn may have less responsibility.

The boundary must still be communicated humanely and precisely.

60. Working conclusion

Fogo Island Inn's public departure evidence is unusually meaningful in two places.

First, it includes a departure lunch pack.

That is a small but concrete acknowledgement that the guest still needs care after leaving the building.

Second, it describes valet service as including departure ferry coordination.

That recognizes that an island hotel's service problem does not stop at the driveway.

Together, these promises suggest a wider departure model:

Room Closure
→
Account Closure
→
Body and Luggage Readiness
→
Transport Handoff
→
External Journey Support
→
Onward Independence

The public evidence does not yet prove the internal system required to deliver that model.

It does not reveal:

checkout time,

final-account review,

luggage custody,

lunch-pack mechanics,

ferry-monitoring ownership,

disruption recovery,

or the precise point at which responsibility transfers.

The strongest departure finding is therefore a boundary:

The Stay Ends When the Room Is Released.

Hospitality Responsibility Ends Only When the Guest Reaches
the Next Usable Journey State the Institution Agreed to Carry Them To.

For a self-driving guest, that point may occur relatively early.

For a guest using Inn-arranged transport to Gander, it may occur much later.

For a charter guest, it may depend on a verified air-operator handoff.

The correct boundary is not universal.

It is explicit, route-dependent and consequence-aware.

This finding does not promote a KHDS pattern.

It establishes a hotel-transfer architecture that later cases must test.
