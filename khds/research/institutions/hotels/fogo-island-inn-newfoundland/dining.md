1. Purpose of this file

This file studies dining as a continuous hospitality system across a multi-day stay at Fogo Island Inn.

It does not treat dining as one restaurant visit.

It studies a food relationship distributed across:

the welcome snack,

Daybreak,

breakfast,

lunch,

supper,

the Dining Room,

the Tea Room,

the 24-hour In-Between menu,

room service,

packed hiking lunches,

The Shed,

bars and lounges,

culinary activities,

and restaurants elsewhere on Fogo Island.

The stay is full-board.

That means food is not merely an optional add-on to the room.

It is one of the principal systems through which the Inn must sustain:

bodily care,

dietary safety,

daily rhythm,

guest agency,

place interpretation,

social choice,

economic relationship,

and trust.

The public language repeatedly connects food with:

nature,

culture,

fishers,

farmers,

hunters,

foragers,

makers,

community businesses,

seasonal change,

and local foodways.

Those connections are important.

They are not self-validating.

The purpose of this file is to identify:

how the dining system changes across the day,

how one dietary record should travel across food surfaces,

how inclusion is made clear at the point of use,

how seasonal variability coexists with reliable care,

how the Inn distributes dining beyond its own building,

what current public evidence proves,

what it leaves unknown,

and which KHDS patterns survive this longer and more distributed food journey.

2. Dining boundary

Dining begins before the first formal meal.

It can begin with:

dietary disclosure during reservation,

pre-arrival planning,

a welcome snack,

or the first conversation about food after arrival.

It continues through every moment when nourishment, food choice or culinary participation affects the stay.

It does not end when the guest leaves the Dining Room.

It can continue:

in the room,

on a trail,

beside a fire,

in the Tea Room,

at The Shed,

at a local restaurant,

or through preparation for the next day.

Fogo Island Inn has a distributed dining system rather than one restaurant product.

Full-board operates across time, location and social mode.

The welcome snack and Daybreak begin dining before formal breakfast.

The In-Between menu and room service extend food outside standard meal periods.

The public evidence does not clarify whether those two services are identical.

Hiking lunches move food into the guest's itinerary.

The Tea Room combines food and human service access.

The Shed offers a deliberately communal mode.

Room service offers a private mode.

Community dining offers an independent off-property mode.

The guest can vary social exposure across dining contexts.

Seasonal menus pressure Stable Participation / Variable Product.

Locality operates at island, provincial, national and trading-partner scales.

Supplier mapping provides bounded system proof.

Economic Nutrition provides stay-level economic proof, not dish-level proof.

Preservation makes time part of the seasonal food system.

“Every dish tells a story” requires optional, usable evidence.

Dietary disclosure must become one cross-surface operational memory.

Community dining is stronger relationship proof than a referral list.

The room number becomes a portable service credential.

Independent restaurant agency must remain visible.

Non-guest Dining Room access is real but capacity-controlled.

The hotel age rule and non-guest dining age rule differ.

Inclusive pricing can reduce transaction friction and increase consumption pressure.

Dining continuity—not menu prestige—is the decisive institutional test.
