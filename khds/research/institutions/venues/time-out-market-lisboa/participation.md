1. Purpose and participation boundary

arrival.md asks whether someone reaches the right operation, counter or receiving point. Participation starts when they can use that route: compare eateries, seek a dietary answer, order, use shared seating, join a specific food-hall event, attend an Academy workshop, or follow an agreed group arrangement. It can start with browsing and may pause, branch or repeat; food purchase is not the only meaningful use of this food-and-cultural venue. Time Out advertises multiple eateries, bars, shops, events, a cooking Academy and a Studio, but advertising those possibilities does not prove each was usable on a particular visit. Time Out Market overview, Academy overview, Events & Groups.

How can a visitor remain free to move between the market's different offers while understanding which operator owns each transaction, where shared resources come from, what an entitlement permits and how a failed route is recovered?

This file analyses possible states and published participation rules. It does not claim to have observed counter queuing, payment settlement, food collection, accessibility, admission, staff handoffs or guest satisfaction. The public-evidence scope and Case #24 in-progress state do not change when this dimension is completed.

Choice ≠ Accepted Order
Accepted Order ≠ Available Communal Seat
Market Entry ≠ Workshop or Event Entitlement

Participation gives way to departure.md once a person has stopped using the venue and must resolve remaining obligations, possessions, refunds or travel. The handoff may recur if a guest leaves one activity and returns to another; this is a model, not an observed circulation policy.

3. Food-led participation: a branching, not mandatory, loop

The market encourages exploring a range of eateries; the older FAQ presents casual communal seating. A hypothesised food-led loop is: inspect current counters and offers; confirm compatibility if needed; choose and place an operator-specific order; understand the accepted payment and wait; receive the product under that operator's actual collection rule; locate/use suitable shared seating; eat, browse again or leave. The publicly inspected pages do not establish that all counters use the same queue, pickup device, receipt, service time or refund process. Market overview, eatery directory, FAQ.

The guest might instead find a seat before ordering, compare options without buying, move to a bar, separate from companions temporarily, join a food-hall event, or visit a shop. None of those branches should be described as an exception to a universal food purchase sequence; their live accessibility and rules remain to be checked. Time Out's overview explicitly invites visitors to browse and try more than one offer. Market overview.

4. Shared seating and multi-counter coordination

The FAQ's communal-table proposition supports flexible walk-in dining but leaves the seat resource distinct from the food order. A group can, in principle, select different eateries while aiming to eat together; that is a plausible use of a multi-operator market, not evidence of a guaranteed coordinated service. Whether people may hold seats before purchase, join or leave tables, move food between areas, receive accessibility-priority seating or get help when the hall is full is not established by the FAQ. FAQ, market food overview.

The coordination problem is not just one queue: three guests ordering at three counters may face three product waits while needing one shared place to sit. The market-level promise is social choice; the operator-level orders and venue-level seating must be made legible without inventing a single owner of every order.

Three separate purchases can feed one shared dining occasion while their fulfilment clocks differ. That relationship is an analytical model, not a report that named vendors or the bar share receipts, pickup systems or staff. It puts specific pressure on Resource Waiting versus Product Waiting and on capacity-object separation; it creates no new formal waiting category.

5. Money and instruments: a current-rule conflict

Time Out's dated 2019 FAQ says it does not accept cash and directs visitors to card payment or a rechargeable card. The currently inspected English market homepage embeds a different FAQ excerpt saying cash is accepted as well as credit/debit cards. These conflicting official presentations cannot be flattened into a reliable venue-wide payment rule for a future visit, and neither establishes what a particular counter accepts today. Payment acceptance and recovery are live, counter-specific questions. 2019 standalone FAQ, English market homepage FAQ excerpt.

Published general TOM card terms describe buying/loading a prepaid card at Time Out Shop on piso 0, using it in participating spaces of the market of purchase and conditions for loss, balance and expiry. These are the issuer's written instrument terms, not observed counter acceptance or a substitute for an eatery order. The Events & Groups page separately presents pre-loaded event cards for groups; the pages reviewed do not demonstrate that the group arrangement shares the general card's full terms or issue/recovery processes. 6. Cultural, workshop and group participation

Food-hall public event. The Portuguese Salsa page describes a free-entry dance activity in the food hall. A guest may want to eat, dance, watch, or do both; the publication does not show which parts of the floor change use, whether seating remains available, who manages crowding or how a visitor with a mobility/sensory need joins. Its posted event information is edition-specific and must be checked afresh; free entry to this event does not define all future events. Salsa no Mercado, market events overview.

Studio performance/event. The Studio is described upstairs and can host various programmes. Participation here requires the actual occurrence's entry rule, location, time and ticket/admission state. The public Studio description is capacity and possibility evidence, not a public entitlement or proof of a working step-free transition, staffed check-in or successful participation. Studio description, Events & Groups.

Academy workshop. Time Out describes learning with chef Miguel Mesquita and the team, and the workshop store separates purchasable editions from unavailable/coming-soon courses. Sample course terms indicate personal-ticket, language, age, included-food, dietary-contact and late-entry conditions; these cannot be silently applied to every course. The market homepage advertises Academy dining seating for 22, which is facility presentation, not a verified inventory for every Academy workshop or private hire. No enrolment or lesson was tested. Academy overview, workshop store, sample masterclass terms, market homepage Academy description.

Private group or hire. The events page advertises Studio, Academy, market buyout and group cards through enquiry. The group organiser and an individual participant can hold different information: one may know payment/room terms; the other needs an accurate meeting point, entitlement and support contact. A group attendee cannot infer they may enter a Studio programme, use every counter or receive dinner simply because the employer booked “the market.” No accepted group agreement was reviewed. Events & Groups.

Role shifts matter: browsing → diner, diner → food-hall dancer, food-hall attendee → Studio attendee, voucher holder → enrolled learner, organiser → hosted group participant. Some shifts may require no payment; others need an edition, ticket or agreement. Many offers ≠ one interchangeable admission state. These shifts are a KHDS hypothesis to test against actual guest routes, not confirmed behaviour.

8. Inclusion and pressure-free optionality

A visitor may browse without buying, eat without attending an event, attend a relevant free activity without enrolling in the Academy, or participate as a companion rather than as the purchasing organiser. This is a route-separation principle for evaluation: the public presentation shows multiple offers, but observed pressure, exclusions, purchases and social outcomes are unknown. A free Salsa listing is not evidence that a person can always participate independently of spending or that every event is accessible to them. Market overview, Salsa description.

The digital accessibility statement does not prove usable seating, step-free Studio access, Academy teaching accommodations, quiet alternatives or accessible queue and payment interaction. Those require physical and role-specific tests. Avoid distributing a person's dietary or mobility disclosures across unrelated operators when a named responsible contact can answer; this is an ethical research test, not a statement about Time Out's current data practices. Digital accessibility statement, Studio floor, FAQ dietary referral.

9. Transfer test against Reffen and governance

Reffen's Case #23 participation research tests a dispersed outdoor set of stall, event, group, leisure and weather-dependent states. Time Out tests an indoor curated market with a traditional-market neighbour, communal dining, upstairs Studio, cooking Academy and written card terms. Recurrence lies in the separation of operator product, shared venue resource and specific programme entitlement, not in identical payment, seating or accessibility rules. 10. Evidence boundary and next handoff

Supported public facts: multiple food/drink and cultural routes; older no-ordinary-table-reservations/communal-seating statement; Academy courses and edition-specific sales; first-floor Studio; sample free-entry food-hall event; separately published general TOM card and advertised group cards. Market overview, FAQ, Academy store, Studio, Salsa, card terms, Events & Groups.

Hypotheses: branching food loop, role changes, cross-counter coordination, separate responsibility objects and recoverable human handoffs. These are architecture interpretations, not Time Out policy or verified guest behaviour.

Unverified: actual counter payment/collection methods; current cash/card/cardholder acceptance; shared-seat allocation and suitability; live queue length and service clock; Academy attendance/teaching outcomes; specific event admission and accessibility; group card identity and event contract terms; dietary safety, human-help performance, privacy and causal guest effects.
