1. Purpose and central question

How can visual evidence help a visitor distinguish the historic building, a curated hall, named food operators, shared seating, a ground-floor cooking school and a first-floor performance space without implying one photographed moment is the whole guest experience?

Time Out publishes visual material on its market landing page, arrival guide, food directory, individual chef and restaurant pages, event pages, Academy store and Studio page. The publicly retrievable pages show image labels, photographer credits and textual context of varying specificity. This audit uses those publisher-provided labels, captions and page contexts; it did not examine original image files, crop variants, embedded metadata, consent records, licensing agreements or responsive rendering. Nor was an in-person market state photographed. Market homepage, arrival/location guide, restaurants guide, Studio page.

The question is about claim-fit visual proof, not whether the market's imagery is attractive. Images may aid a choice when they give the viewer the right subject, location, operator, moment and limitation. An attractive frame cannot independently establish service performance, food safety, authentic sourcing, physical accessibility, visitor comfort or guest emotions.

Visible Subject ≠ Entire Operating System
Photographed Dish ≠ Current Offer
Photographed Person ≠ Verified Experience

2. Method: what was, and was not, inspected

The desk audit inspected Time Out's official public pages for retrievable image subjects/labels and surrounding copy, then compared the apparent claim with a visitor's decision. Labels such as “Fachada do edifício,” “Food hall do Time Out Market Lisboa,” named dishes and “Estúdio Time Out” are page-published descriptions, not a visual verification that the rendered picture contains every relevant route or feature. Some gallery frames are named only “Time Out Market Lisboa”; others have more specific dish/person labels. Location guide, food guide, Chef Marlene Vieira, Marisqueira Azul, market gallery.

This research did not:

download and visually inspect the full official gallery or verify embedded EXIF dates;

test alt text in a screen reader or verify every image's accessible name;

confirm whether an image depicts a typical, current, staged or archived moment;

compare cropped mobile frames with desktop originals;

obtain image permissions, releases or operator-specific reuse terms;

observe live arrivals, queues, seating, show admission or cooking classes;

infer food and event availability from page imagery.

Photographer credits visible on several pages (for example, the façade, food hall and restaurant galleries) are attribution cues, not a record of usage rights or subject consent. Location guide, food guide, Chef Marlene Vieira.

4. The five visual-proof boundaries

4.1 Frame and location

An exterior photo can provide a landmark, yet it may crop out another entrance, a kerb, the route to the Academy, a lift or a temporary obstacle. A food-hall-wide image can show only the photographed part of the room. Time Out locates the Academy on the ground floor and the Studio on the first floor, but no inspected photograph demonstrates a continuous usable path from street to both spaces. Use a route diagram, validated accessibility information and staff guidance where a static frame is insufficient. Location guide, Academy FAQ, Studio page.

4.2 Operator and product

The chef and seafood galleries are attached to named pages, unlike a general market gallery. That context lets a viewer connect one operator to a photographed dish, but does not establish its menu status, producer relationship, dietary suitability or meal served at a future visit. Brand-level curation is an editorial selection claim; it is not a blanket photographic certificate for every operator. Chef Marlene Vieira, Marisqueira Azul, Time Out curation FAQ.

4.3 Time and state

The food guide displays an editorial date of 1 October 2024, while the current Academy store distinguishes “Out of Stock,” “Coming Soon” and other workshop purchase states. A photo displayed with an older guide cannot substitute for today's seller roster or menu; a class image cannot make a “Coming Soon” workshop purchasable. Archive/preview/current states need their own timestamps and labels. Dated food guide, Academy store.

Similarly, an event promo image and an event recap photograph, if one exists, serve different time claims. The events index lists dated programmes; the Salsa language versions conflict on recurring weekday and portions of time. Photos cannot arbitrate which occurrence actually runs or guarantee its admission. Events index, Portuguese Salsa, English Salsa, Case #24 events research.

4.4 Claim scale

One seller's plate is one seller's possible example. One photographed common table is not all seating conditions or a seat inventory. Studio imagery is not the dining hall's ordinary atmosphere; neither is a hall concert a routine meal state. Images have to disclose their operator, space and event/ordinary-service scope, particularly when attached to venue-wide promises. Portuguese FAQ on shared seating, Studio, Marisqueira Azul.

4.5 Causality and personhood

A photo of a chef, worker, dancer, child in a workshop or diner, if published, can show visible presence and action at a moment. It cannot establish their employment conditions, culinary authorship beyond the stated claim, consent to renewed promotion, learning outcome, enjoyment, safety or whether a person belongs. The Academy's official store advertises children's workshops, but the public programme is not evidence of how children's identities and images are protected. Chef page, Academy classes, Salsa listing.

6. Editorial curation versus distributed authorship

The institution frames its vendors as a Time Out editorially selected city offer, while individual restaurant pages name operators, chefs, dishes and sometimes photographers. A photograph's authorship has at least three separate axes: who made the image, whose food/work is depicted, and who controls the surrounding brand claim. Those roles cannot be collapsed into “Time Out made every food experience.” Time Out selection account, restaurant guide, Chef Marlene Vieira, Marisqueira Azul.

To demonstrate difference, the meaningful visual relationship is:

market's selection frame → named seller → actual counter/product/process → shared guest route, each supported by a caption or other evidence appropriate to the claim. Merely photographing many plates proves variety of visible dishes; it does not explain why those specific sellers were chosen or how a diner accesses them. This is a falsifiable editorial-proof hypothesis, not a claim that existing imagery fully achieves it. Food guide, selection FAQ.

Also keep historic Mercado da Ribeira, its continuing traditional wing and the modern Time Out hall distinct. The older market's building photograph can support place context; it is not itself sourcing proof or an image of continuous ownership. Time Out venue history.

7. Accessibility, alt text and ethical visual evidence

The inspected pages include some specifically labelled image subjects (façade, food hall, chef/dish, Studio) and a venue gallery with repeated broad “Time Out Market Lisboa” labels. This variation is observable in the retrievable page presentation; it does not constitute a complete automated accessibility audit. A generic venue label may not tell a nonvisual visitor whether a frame shows a door, table, operator or performer. An image labelled “façade” is still not a textual route to the right entrance. Venue gallery, arrival guide, restaurant galleries, Studio.

Design tests for later audit:

Can a screen-reader user get the same decision value from meaningful labels, directions, operator text and event-state descriptions, rather than decorative alt text?

Do close crops omit the route, table clearance or counter identity used to support a decision?

Does a mobility-related claim rely on a continuous usable route, not a cropped photo of one ramp or floor?

Are identifiable adults, workers, performers and children covered by role- and purpose-specific consent for each reuse? No releases were reviewed here.

Does an event crowd image preserve location, occurrence and a privacy-compatible frame instead of implying everyone endorsed the institution?

The publisher's digital accessibility statement addresses website access; it does not verify physical passage through the market or image-by-image usability. No physical access, legal compliance or consent outcome is asserted.

Reffen's reused-container visual novelty differs from Time Out's building/curation emphasis; in both, an image should prove the guest-relevant relationship among place, seller, shared space and moment, not just provide spectacle. 9. Falsification and required next evidence

To test the proposals rather than assume them:

Inspect actual gallery bitmaps and responsive crops, image dates, captions, photographer/licensing metadata and operator consent agreements.

Walk the visitor routes to hall, traditional wing, Academy and Studio with a qualified physical-access review; compare the photos' indicated route with a real usable route.

Match named operator images to current offers and evaluate whether product/counter/author relationships remain legible without a general brand claim.

Sample normal and programmed time states, preserving event dates and photographing only with authorised permission.

Test screen-reader names and functional textual alternatives for decisions on entering, eating, learning and attending a show.

Interview willing participants about what the visuals actually helped them understand, keeping their account distinct from marketing claims.

Check whether disclosure of operator, time and event state reduces confusion; if not, revise the proposed visual-proof sequence.

None of these studies has been performed in this file.
