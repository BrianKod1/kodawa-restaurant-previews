1. Purpose of this file

This file studies dining at Reffen as a distributed hospitality system.

Reffen presents one destination to the guest.

The meal, however, may be produced through several independent components:

a venue-level discovery system,

an independently operated food stall,

a separate bar,

a stall-specific menu,

a stall-specific queue,

a stall-specific payment and preparation sequence,

a venue-level gift-card or group-card mechanism,

shared seating,

shared shelter,

common-area cleaning,

and different recovery owners when something fails.

Dining cannot therefore be evaluated as if Reffen were one restaurant with one kitchen, one menu, one service team and one table inventory.

The file asks how a coherent guest meal can emerge from a hospitality environment whose product creation is deliberately plural.

2. Dining boundary

For this case, dining begins when a guest starts trying to answer:

What can I eat or drink here, which operator can provide it, and what must I do to receive and consume it safely and appropriately?

Dining includes:

cuisine and vendor discovery,

menu interpretation,

price interpretation,

dietary screening,

allergen enquiry,

vendor selection,

ordering,

payment,

preparation waiting,

collection,

condiment and utensil access,

seat finding,

group meal coordination,

eating and drinking,

clearing or disposal,

complaint routing,

and recovery.

Dining does not include every activity available at Reffen.

Events, games, workshops, nightlife and creative-stall use are governed primarily in participation.md unless they materially change the meal.

3. Primary research question

How does one guest-facing venue support coherent dining when food, drinks, timing, product knowledge and transaction responsibility are distributed across independent operators?

5. Research method

This file uses public evidence from:

Reffen's official food directory,

Reffen's official practical-information page,

Reffen's official group-booking page,

Reffen's official bar directory,

Reffen's official institutional story,

selected official vendor profiles,

the official food-stall application page,

and the preceding Case #23 files.

The research separates:

Published fact — directly stated by an official source.

Architecture inference — a system relationship strongly implied by public facts.

Research hypothesis — a mechanism requiring observation or operational evidence.

Unverified outcome — a result that cannot be claimed from public evidence.

No order was placed.

No vendor was contacted.

No live queue, kitchen, payment terminal, collection process, seating condition or waste flow was observed.

No food-quality, dietary-safety or guest-satisfaction outcome is verified.

6. Verified public dining facts

The current official surfaces establish that:

Reffen describes its food stalls as independent businesses led by chefs and entrepreneurs.

The venue presents food from many geographic and culinary traditions.

The current food directory supports text search and filters labelled gluten free, halal, lactose-free, vegan and vegetarian.

Individual vendor profiles can include an operator story, a cuisine framing, a signature dish, photographs, dietary labels, social links and a Danish food-inspection link.

The practical-information page instructs guests to ask the relevant stall about halal, kosher, organic or similar requirements.

The same page says many stalls have vegetarian and vegan dishes and may adjust dishes.

Outside food and drink are generally not permitted.

Food stalls and bars accept credit cards and cash in Danish kroner.

Reffen gift cards are described as usable across all food stalls and bars.

Group organisers can arrange individual Reffen cards with chosen stored values and consolidate payment into one bill.

Group bookings remain self-service for food and drinks unless a separately described arrangement applies.

Shared covered dining is available in Beer Garden and Werkstatt.

The official application page describes shared operator infrastructure including a payment system, water, drains, power, a common preparation kitchen, a common warehouse, a high-temperature washing station, storage and cleaning of common areas.

Food-stall applicants are expected to hold a hygiene certificate.

Applicant assessment asks about production, products, prices, suppliers, ingredients, sustainability, signage, storytelling and marketing.

These facts establish a dining architecture.

They do not establish consistent live performance.

7. One venue / many dining contracts

The guest can experience Reffen as one place while entering separate transactions with different operators.

One Venue Identity

≠

One Dining Contract

At minimum, the guest may interact with:

Reffen as venue curator and common-infrastructure provider,

one food-stall operator as meal seller and producer,

one bar operator or venue bar as drink seller,

a gift-card or group-card system as payment infrastructure,

and shared seating as a venue resource.

The dining experience is unified perceptually and distributed operationally.

This distinction is the central pressure of the file.

Reffen's public food promise is based on:

cultural and personal roots,

street-food informality,

choice across tastes and dietary categories,

shared social use,

and a harbour-side cultural environment.

The venue does not promise one signature menu.

It promises access to a changing collection of specific operators.

The primary venue product is therefore not one dish.

9. Vendor-level dining promise

Each vendor can present a narrower promise based on:

a cuisine,

a place of origin,

a cooking method,

a signature product,

These pages show that product discovery may be carried through biography, place, method and signature dish rather than menu enumeration alone.

The operator profile is therefore part of the dining interface.

Public menu visibility is distributed across:

photographs,

dietary tags,

operator social channels,

physical stall signage,

15. Product variability

Variation can occur at several levels:

the stall roster may change by season,

operating stalls may vary by day or time,

individual dishes may change,

ingredients may be unavailable,

preparation capacity may change with queues,

and menu suitability may change through recipe or cross-contact conditions.

Reffen's stable dining promise is therefore not a fixed product catalogue.

The stronger stable layer appears to be:

access to a curated multi-vendor environment,

a common discovery surface,

known payment routes,

shared seating and shelter,

and operator-level confirmation.

Stable Dining Participation

can coexist with

Variable Vendor Product

This pressures the existing Candidate Stable Participation / Variable Product without changing its maturity.

16. Choice is an active dining task

In a conventional restaurant, a guest usually chooses dishes after choosing the restaurant.

At Reffen, the guest may need to choose:

whether to dine,

which cuisine or product category matters,

which operator appears compatible,

whether the physical queue is acceptable,

whether the item is available,

whether the price is acceptable,

whether dietary requirements can be met,

whether the wait fits the group,

and where the resulting meal can be consumed.

Vendor choice is not a prelude to dining.

It is part of dining.

The venue shifts more selection work to the guest while offering greater breadth.

17. Variety and decision burden

Variety creates value when it increases relevant choice.

It creates burden when the guest cannot compare options efficiently.

Possible burdens include:

scanning many stalls,

translating unfamiliar cuisine names,

comparing incomplete menus,

locating compatible dietary options,

checking multiple queues,

coordinating different preferences,

and re-routing after a choice fails.

The current directory reduces some of this burden through search, filters and vendor stories.

It does not remove the need for physical confirmation.

More Choice

≠

More Usable Choice

The relevant measure is not vendor count alone.

It is the number of understandable and currently fulfilable options for the particular guest.

19. Dietary label hierarchy

A KHDS dining interface should distinguish at least four evidence levels:

Level 1 — Venue category

The venue indicates that relevant options may exist.

Level 2 — Vendor attribute

The vendor is tagged as offering one or more relevant categories.

Level 3 — Dish attribute

A specific current dish is described as meeting a defined requirement.

Level 4 — Preparation assurance

The operator explains ingredients, procedures, cross-contact risk and the limits of any accommodation.

Reffen's public website is strongest at Levels 1 and 2.

Public evidence is incomplete at Levels 3 and 4.

21. Outside-food rule

Reffen states that guests may not bring their own food and drink and connects the rule to supporting independent stallholders.

This rule protects the venue's commercial ecosystem.

It can also create a compatibility boundary.

If a guest cannot identify a safe, medically appropriate, culturally appropriate or age-appropriate option, the rule may remove the guest's recovery route.

Public evidence reviewed here does not establish exceptions for:

or other necessary accommodations.

30. Equality of value and equality of access

Giving every group member the same stored value may appear equal.

The practical purchasing power may differ because:

compatible vendors may have different prices,

portion needs may differ,

a guest may need food and a non-alcoholic drink,

a dietary restriction may narrow choice,

queues may impose different time costs,

or a companion may need assistance ordering and collecting.

≠

33. Collection

Self-service transfers a material handoff to the guest.

The guest must often:

hear or recognise readiness,

return to the correct operator,

identify the correct order,

carry the product through a busy shared environment,

and relocate companions or seating.

Collection is therefore not a trivial final step.

It is a mobility, memory and communication task.

It can fail when:

the readiness signal is inaccessible,

the guest leaves auditory range,

the order identifier is unclear,

pathways are crowded,

carrying requires more capacity than the guest has,

or the table cannot be found again.

34. Shared seating

Food purchase and seating appear to be separate objects.

A guest can buy food from one operator and consume it in a venue-level shared area.

The transaction may secure the food without securing a seat.

For a group booking, the reserved area can reduce the second uncertainty without fixing the first.

51. Dining failure states

51.1 Desired vendor closed

The guest reaches a listed operator that is not currently serving.

51.2 Desired item unavailable

The vendor is open but the relevant product is sold out.

51.3 Price mismatch

The live price differs from the guest's expectation.

51.4 Dietary ambiguity

A directory tag suggests relevance but the operator cannot confirm the required dish or preparation condition.

51.5 No compatible option

The guest cannot identify food that meets a necessary restriction and outside food is prohibited.

51.6 Payment failure

The selected payment route is declined, unavailable or not accepted as expected.

51.7 Group-card insufficiency

Stored value does not cover the selected purchase.

51.8 Excessive queue

The vendor is technically available but not usable within the guest's time or mobility constraints.

51.9 Missed readiness signal

The guest does not hear, see or understand that the order is ready.

51.10 Wrong order

The collected product does not match the transaction.

51.11 Unsafe or incompatible product

The served item conflicts with the confirmed requirement.

51.12 Carrying failure

The guest cannot safely move the food or drink to a consumption place.

51.13 No suitable seat

The guest obtains food but cannot find an appropriate table or sheltered space.

51.14 Group timing failure

Independent orders arrive too far apart to support a coherent shared meal.

51.15 Disposal uncertainty

The guest cannot identify how or where to clear the meal materials.

51.16 Responsibility bounce

Venue and vendor routes each direct the guest elsewhere without resolution.

52. Recovery hierarchy

A useful recovery sequence is:

Detect Failure
→
Identify Affected Object
→
Identify Responsible Layer
→
Provide Immediate Route
→
Preserve Payment and Dietary Context
→
Resolve / Replace / Refund / Re-route
→
Restore Dining Participation

Examples:

sold-out item → vendor substitution or new vendor choice;

payment failure → alternate payment or payment-system support;

wrong dish → vendor correction;

dietary incompatibility → safe re-selection or explicit inability to serve;

no seating → venue-level seating guidance;

inaccessible collection → human assistance;

card problem → venue or group-card support;

unclear waste route → common-area staff guidance.

Recovery should preserve dignity and avoid forcing the guest to repeat sensitive dietary or accessibility information unnecessarily.

53. Dining completion

Dining is not complete when payment succeeds.

It is complete when the relevant guest can:

receive the intended product,

understand any unresolved safety limits,

consume it in an appropriate environment,

complete clearing or disposal,

and access recovery when the contract is not fulfilled.

For a group, completion may also require:

the members to reunite,

sufficient seating,

manageable service-time divergence,

and clear treatment of unused or insufficient card value.

60. Falsification programme

60.1 Curated choice environment

Hypothesis: Venue-level curation materially helps guests find a suitable meal.

Could be falsified if: Guests rely almost entirely on physical trial-and-error and the venue directory does not affect selection.

Needed evidence: Journey observation, search analytics and guest interviews.

60.2 Vendor-profile utility

Hypothesis: Founder, place and signature-dish information improves vendor comprehension.

Could be falsified if: It does not affect understanding or choice and displaces more important current menu information.

Needed evidence: Comprehension testing and decision studies.

60.3 Dietary filters

Hypothesis: Venue-level filters reduce search burden without being interpreted as safety guarantees.

Could be falsified if: Filters are materially stale, misunderstood or inconsistent with vendor practice.

Needed evidence: Tag audit, operator interviews and guest interpretation testing.

60.4 Operator confirmation

Hypothesis: Guests can obtain reliable current compatibility information at the stall.

Could be falsified if: Staff knowledge, language access, queue pressure or inconsistent procedures prevent reliable answers.

Needed evidence: Scenario testing and documented operator protocols.

60.5 Outside-food recovery

Hypothesis: Necessary exceptions can be handled without excluding guests or undermining vendor support.

Could be falsified if: No usable exception route exists for guests whose needs cannot be met internally.

Needed evidence: Current policy and case handling.

60.6 Shared-card autonomy

Hypothesis: Individual Reffen cards preserve personal choice while reducing organiser administration.

Could be falsified if: Balance, top-up or acceptance problems materially constrain users.

Needed evidence: Card terms, support data and group observation.

60.7 Multi-clock dining

Hypothesis: Independent vendor timing creates a material group-coordination burden.

Could be falsified if: Preparation times are sufficiently short or groups naturally absorb the variation without cost.

Needed evidence: Timed observations and group interviews.

60.8 Seat / purchase decoupling

Hypothesis: Separate food and seat capacity creates meaningful risk during busy states.

Could be falsified if: Available seating consistently absorbs peak demand or staff effectively allocate space.

Needed evidence: Occupancy observation across weather and demand states.

60.9 Human assistance

Hypothesis: Visible cross-operator assistance materially improves dining completion for guests who cannot use self-service independently.

Could be falsified if: Assistance is unavailable, unclear or cannot cross the venue/vendor boundary.

Needed evidence: Accessibility walkthroughs and recovery scenarios.

60.10 Responsibility routing

Hypothesis: Object-based routing reduces guest effort during failure.

Could be falsified if: Venue and vendors resolve issues through one already-clear common route.

Needed evidence: Complaint mapping and service-recovery testing.

60.11 Shared infrastructure proof

Hypothesis: Publishing common infrastructure meaningfully increases confidence in the dining system.

Could be falsified if: Guests do not understand its relevance or it creates unjustified confidence in vendor outcomes.

Needed evidence: Comprehension and trust calibration testing.

60.12 Waste completion

Hypothesis: Clear return and sorting instructions improve dining completion and reduce common-area disorder.

Could be falsified if: Staff-led clearing makes guest instructions unnecessary or the proposed route increases friction.

Needed evidence: Current operations, waste audit and observational testing.
