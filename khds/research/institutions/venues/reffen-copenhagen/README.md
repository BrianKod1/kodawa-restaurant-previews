1. Case declaration

Reffen is KHDS Case #23.

It is the third non-restaurant institution case in the KHDS Cases #21–30 research cycle.

Case #21 studied Fogo Island Inn as a multi-day hotel and destination-hospitality system.

Case #22 studied Sky Lagoon as a timed wellness and ritual-participation system.

Case #23 studies an open-air street-food market and cultural venue where the guest experiences one destination while hospitality is delivered through:

the venue operator,

independent food-stall operators,

bars and a coffee venue,

event and nightlife environments,

shared seating and circulation areas,

transport operators,

parking and mooring operators,

and human assistance distributed across the site.

The official website describes Reffen as a seasonal street-food market and cultural destination on Refshaleøen. It presents independent chefs and artisans, a changing food offer, bars, events, workshops, family activities, reused industrial structures and the connected Werkstatt environment.

This makes Reffen a useful transfer case because:

One Guest-Facing Place

≠

One Hospitality Operator

The case asks whether KHDS can preserve legibility, participation, proof and recovery when responsibility is distributed but the guest may perceive the experience as one whole.

2. Research purpose

The purpose of Case #23 is not to decide whether Reffen is a good food market.

It is not a review.

It is not an endorsement.

It is not a ranking of Reffen's food stalls, bars, events or facilities.

It does not treat Reffen's published ambitions as verified guest outcomes.

The purpose is to study the institution's publicly documented hospitality architecture and test whether KHDS patterns remain useful when:

entry is generally open and free,

most individual participation is walk-in,

food choice is distributed across independent stalls,

tables and common areas are shared,

individual vendor inventory may change independently,

the venue programme changes across days and seasons,

weather affects the usable hospitality environment,

the site combines dining, drinking, culture, leisure and nightlife,

the guest may transact with several operators during one visit,

and the visible venue boundary may not match the operational responsibility boundary.

The research therefore asks:

How should a multi-operator hospitality venue create legible arrival, fair participation, coherent atmosphere, sufficient proof and recoverable responsibility when the guest experiences one place but service is distributed across independent vendors, bars, events and venue operators?

3. Why this institution was selected

Reffen was selected for contradiction and transfer value rather than prestige.

Its value to KHDS comes from the interaction of eight pressures.

3.1 Shared-venue pressure

Reffen presents a common destination identity.

The guest may nevertheless interact with:

multiple food businesses,

several bars,

a coffee venue,

venue staff,

event operators,

Workshop or Werkstatt environments,

and external transport or parking systems.

The public-facing place is unified.

The operating system is distributed.

This creates the principal boundary:

Shared Venue

≠

Shared Responsibility for Every Transaction

3.2 Open-entry pressure

The official practical-information page states that admission to Reffen is generally free, including many concerts and similar events.

It also states that some Werkstatt events may be ticketed.

Most visitors can arrive without a reservation.

Open entry does not establish:

an available table,

an available preferred dish,

a short queue,

accessible circulation during peak periods,

admission to every event,

or successful participation through every intended route.

The transfer boundary is:

Open Entry

≠

Guaranteed Participation

3.3 Distributed-product pressure

Reffen's official food directory presents many independent stalls serving different cuisines and dietary possibilities.

The venue-level product is variety.

The transaction-level product belongs to a particular stall.

This raises questions about:

menu freshness,

vendor opening state,

stock availability,

dietary information,

allergy responsibility,

payment and refund ownership,

and failure recovery.

The boundary is:

Venue-Level Variety

≠

Vendor-Level Availability

3.4 Multi-intent pressure

The official website presents Reffen as more than a place to buy food.

Published uses include:

eating,

drinking,

meeting,

attending concerts,

visiting markets,

joining workshops,

family participation,

play,

skating,

browsing creative businesses,

and entering Werkstatt nightlife.

These uses may represent materially different guest intents.

The research must distinguish them from a simple list of amenities.

Multiple Offers

≠

Multiple Hospitality Intents

3.5 Seasonal and weather pressure

Reffen publicly describes a summer season from March through September and a Halloween season in October.

It also provides covered alternatives in the Beer Garden and Werkstatt for poor weather.

The operating promise therefore changes with:

season,

day,

time,

event programme,

temperature,

rain,

sunlight,

crowd level,

and vendor state.

This tests whether stable participation can survive a variable environment and product system.

3.6 Crowd-capacity pressure

The official accessibility information states that busy sunny days can create crowds and bottlenecks in food and bar areas.

This is important because the site can remain technically open while a guest's intended route becomes difficult or unavailable.

Capacity must therefore be separated into:

site-entry capacity,

circulation capacity,

accessible-route capacity,

seating capacity,

vendor queue capacity,

vendor stock capacity,

bar capacity,

covered-space capacity,

event capacity,

and human-assistance capacity.

Venue Open

≠

Every Participation Route Available

3.7 Curated-independence pressure

Reffen states that stall and workshop operators are selected for their concepts, quality and skills, and that the venue supports entrepreneurs through guidance, knowledge sharing, common infrastructure, events and marketing.

This creates a layered model:

Venue Curation
→
Shared Infrastructure
→
Independent Operator Delivery
→
One Perceived Guest Experience

The research must determine what coherence can be created without erasing operator independence.

3.8 Evidence-freshness pressure

The official practical-information page contains internally inconsistent quantities and hours.

Examples observed on 2026-09-14 include:

approximately 35 food stalls in one section and more than 30 in the FAQ;

approximately 10 bars in one section and eight bars plus a coffee venue in the FAQ;

food-stall closing at 20:30 in the primary opening-hours section and 21:30 in an expanded FAQ passage;

bar closing at 21:30 in the primary section and 22:00 in the FAQ passage.

The research must preserve these discrepancies rather than silently select the more convenient answer.

5. Primary research question

How should a multi-operator hospitality venue create legible arrival, fair participation, coherent atmosphere, sufficient proof and recoverable responsibility when the guest experiences one place but service is distributed across independent vendors, bars, events and venue operators?

6. Secondary research questions

6.1 Discovery

Can a visitor understand whether Reffen is primarily a food market, event venue, social destination, family environment or nightlife venue?

Does the website help guests select a route based on intent rather than only browse attractions?

How clearly are seasonal state, current hours, vendor state and event state separated?

Can food variety be demonstrated without implying that every listed stall or dish is always available?

Does the industrial and reused-material story explain the operating model or mainly decorate it?

6.2 Booking and access

When is no reservation the correct participation route?

When does a group table booking become necessary or useful?

What exactly does a booked table secure?

How are free-entry events distinguished from ticketed Werkstatt events?

Can a guest tell which commitment belongs to the venue and which belongs to another operator?

6.3 Pre-arrival

Which information must be checked on the day of the visit?

How should weather, current hours, transport, event state and crowd conditions be presented together?

Can guests with mobility, dietary or sensory requirements prepare without contacting several operators?

What happens when official pages disagree about hours or operator quantities?

Which parts of a visit can be planned, and which remain operationally variable?

6.4 Arrival

Can guests recognise the correct entrance and understand the site's layout?

Does arrival orientation explain food, bars, seating, toilets, covered areas, accessibility and events?

How does the venue support guests who arrive with no chosen vendor?

Where should live changes or closures be disclosed?

Can physical signage preserve independent vendor identity while maintaining venue-level legibility?

6.5 Participation

Is walk-in participation genuinely first-class during crowded periods?

Which queues represent access waiting, product waiting, service waiting or exception waiting?

What does a guest do when the preferred stall is closed, sold out or incompatible with their needs?

Where does structured self-service become insufficient?

How are children, groups, solo guests and people with access needs supported differently without creating rank?

6.6 Dining

Who owns menu accuracy at venue and vendor levels?

How are dietary labels governed across independent businesses?

Can one group combine food from several stalls while retaining a coherent shared meal?

Who handles an incorrect order, refund, allergy concern or unavailable item?

Does shared seating make the meal a venue experience, a vendor experience or both?

6.7 Events and multi-intent use

When does the guest move from market participant to event attendee?

Which events are included in general access and which create a separate entitlement?

How do food and event hours interact?

Can event atmosphere support diners without displacing them?

Does nightlife create a new hospitality state requiring different arrival, safety and departure information?

6.8 Departure and recovery

When does Reffen's hospitality responsibility end?

Can guests easily locate return transport information at departure time?

Who recovers a failed vendor transaction?

Who handles lost property, site-level complaints and event-specific problems?

Can the guest reach the correct responsible party without understanding the operating structure first?

6.9 Atmosphere

Is atmosphere created primarily through food, people, events, waterfront setting, industrial material reuse or self-directed exploration?

Can curatorial coherence exist without standardising independent operators?

How does atmosphere change across weather, crowd and time states?

Is the public promise of welcome supported by usable routes for different guests?

What happens when a lively atmosphere becomes an accessibility or orientation burden?

6.10 Photography and proof

Does photography demonstrate how the venue works or mainly show visual energy?

Can images prove covered space, circulation, shared seating and accessibility?

Can photography represent vendors as independent protagonists rather than venue decoration?

How should seasonal and crowd-state differences be shown?

What evidence is required beyond photography for live availability and operational responsibility?

6.11 Story

Is Reffen's strongest narrative carrier place transformation, entrepreneurship, global food cultures, reuse or public gathering?

Does the venue story clarify why independent operators belong together?

Can global food storytelling avoid reducing cuisines and owners to colourful variety?

Who is the narrative protagonist: Reffen, Refshaleøen, the entrepreneurs, Copenhagen or the guests?

Which claims require evidence beyond the venue's own narrative?

10. Entitlement model

Case #23 will distinguish four primary entry states.

State 1 — General walk-in visitor

The guest may enter the site during published opening conditions.

This does not reserve:

a table,

a product,

a queue position,

covered seating,

or an event place.

State 2 — Group-table holder

The official information describes a bookable covered Beer Garden table for groups of at least eight during defined days and hours.

The stated entitlement is time-limited table use.

Food and drinks remain self-service.

State 3 — Free-event participant

The guest may attend a published event covered by general free entry.

The precise capacity or admission guarantee must not be inferred without event-specific evidence.

State 4 — Ticketed-event participant

A Werkstatt or related event may require a separate ticket.

The ticket may create a different entry condition, time state and responsible operator.

12. Patterns under pressure

Case #23 will test the transferability and limits of the following canonical patterns.

Walk-In as First-Class Participation

Reffen gives walk-in participation structural priority.

The test is whether the route remains usable when crowd, seating, stock, weather or access conditions change.

Multi-Intent Hospitality

The venue supports dining, drinking, events, leisure, family use, creative exploration and nightlife.

The test is whether these represent materially different intents with appropriately different routes.

Hospitality System Proof

Public evidence describes entrepreneur selection and shared infrastructure.

The test is whether the guest can understand how the system affects their own participation and recovery.

Capacity Proof

The venue contains several independent capacity stocks.

The test is whether useful confidence can be created without pretending that one availability indicator describes the whole place.

Stable Participation / Variable Product

The general visit route may remain stable while vendors, menus, events, hours, weather and stock change.

The test is which participation rules are actually stable.

Participation Rule Consistency

Different operators may publish or apply different rules.

The test is whether venue-level orientation can preserve coherence without falsely standardising them.

Human Assistance Where Structured Self-Service Is Insufficient

Ordering and collection are largely self-service.

The test is whether human support becomes visible and correctly routed during exceptions.

Photography as Use-Case Evidence

The venue's multiple guest intents require more than atmosphere imagery.

The test is whether photography demonstrates how different people actually use the site.

Photography Through Layers of Experience

The site can be represented through destination, arrival, circulation, vendor, process, product, seating, events, weather and departure.

The test is whether that sequence produces useful orientation rather than only visual abundance.

Story Through Hospitality Model

Independent entrepreneurship, reuse and curation are part of the published operating model.

The test is whether the model itself explains the experience.

Own the Guest Relationship

Reffen has venue-level communication, group booking and gift-card routes while individual purchases occur across operators.

The case can pressure this partially supported pattern but cannot establish CRM, payment-data, consent or retention ownership from public pages alone.

13. Initial architecture directions

The following are research directions only.

They are not formal KHDS patterns.

13.1 Shared-Venue Responsibility Map

The system may need to reveal the correct responsibility endpoint without exposing irrelevant organisational complexity.

13.2 Venue / Vendor Entitlement Boundary

A venue-level promise should not imply a vendor-level entitlement that has not been created.

13.3 Multi-Stock Capacity

Capacity may need to be represented as several related stocks rather than one venue-wide state.

13.4 Open-Entry Recovery

When entry is open but the intended activity fails, the guest needs a dignified alternative rather than only access to the site.

13.5 Distributed Complaint Routing

The interface may need to route problems by transaction, place, event and infrastructure responsibility.

13.6 Vendor-State Visibility

Venue discovery may need current vendor state without converting independent stalls into identical units.

13.7 Weather-State Hospitality

Weather is not only atmosphere. It can change capacity, comfort, accessibility and participation.

13.8 Curatorial Coherence Without Standardisation

The venue may create shared legibility and values while preserving operator difference.

13.9 Temporary-Operator Continuity

The institution may need to remain recognisable while its vendor and event composition changes.

13.10 Responsibility Legibility Before Failure

Guests should be able to locate help before a problem forces them to infer who owns the route.

15. Evidence and claim discipline

Case #23 must distinguish:

Venue claim — what Reffen says about itself.

Published mechanism — a route, rule, facility or responsibility described by an official source.

Vendor claim — what an individual operator says about its product or practice.

Observed public interface — what can be verified on the current website.

Operational performance — what happens in live service.

Guest experience — what a visitor perceives or feels.

Entrepreneur outcome — what changes for participating operators.

Community or environmental outcome — a wider effect requiring proportional evidence.

The public-evidence dossier can establish the first four when evidence is available.

It cannot establish the final four without appropriate independent or operational evidence.

16. Research boundaries

This case will not:

rank individual vendors,

infer food quality from imagery,

infer hygiene performance from application requirements,

infer dietary safety from category tags,

infer accessibility performance from facility descriptions,

infer inclusion from free admission,

infer belonging from crowd photography,

infer entrepreneur success from access to shared infrastructure,

infer environmental outcomes from material reuse alone,

infer venue responsibility for externally operated parking or mooring,

infer contractual relationships from public-facing language,

or treat one multi-operator venue as proof of cross-venue recurrence.

The research remains bounded to current public evidence.
