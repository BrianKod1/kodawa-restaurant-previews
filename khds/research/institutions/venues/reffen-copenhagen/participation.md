1. Purpose of this file

This file studies how guests use Reffen after reaching a settled arrival state.

Participation is not treated as food purchase alone.

The public venue supports several possible actions:

exploring food stalls,

selecting a vendor,

ordering,

paying,

waiting,

collecting food or drinks,

finding and using shared seating,

eating with people who purchased from different operators,

using a reserved group area,

attending music or cultural events,

joining workshops or markets,

playing games,

using family and leisure facilities,

visiting creative operators,

entering Werkstatt,

and moving between these activities during one visit.

The guest may participate through one action or several.

The venue does not require every visitor to follow one fixed sequence.

That openness creates value.

It also creates coordination questions involving:

intent,

operator identity,

queue ownership,

payment,

product fulfilment,

seating,

timing,

accessibility,

weather,

capacity,

and recovery.

The central participation problem is:

How Can the Guest Remain Free to Explore
While the System Keeps Each Commitment,
Responsibility and Recovery Route Legible?

2. Participation boundary

Participation begins when the visitor can pursue an intended activity without unresolved basic arrival uncertainty.

It can begin through:

browsing without purchasing,

joining a vendor queue,

ordering food or drink,

occupying a reserved group space,

entering an event,

using a free activity,

visiting a creative operator,

or joining social companions.

Participation ends when:

the visitor leaves the relevant activity,

the final transaction is resolved,

the group or event entitlement ends,

or the guest begins departure from the venue.

It can pause and restart several times.

2.1 Arrival boundary

Arrival provides sufficient orientation to begin.

Participation uses that orientation to act.

2.4 Working definition

Participation is the guest's route through one or more venue, vendor, event, social or leisure activities, including the choices, waits, payments, shared resources, role changes and recovery states required to complete them.

3. Primary research question

How should a multi-operator venue preserve guest autonomy across food, drink, groups, events and leisure while keeping capacity, entitlement, operator responsibility and failure recovery understandable?

4. Secondary research questions

4.1 Choice

Can guests compare independent vendors without excessive search?

Does variety support agency or create decision burden?

Can a group split across vendors and still participate together?

How should live closures or sold-out items affect choice?

4.2 Transaction

Who owns the order, payment and fulfilment state?

Can the guest identify the operator after moving away from the stall?

How are gift cards or Reffen cards distinguished from product entitlements?

What happens when payment or collection fails?

4.3 Waiting

Which waits belong to vendors, shared resources, events or exceptions?

Can guests wait without losing their group or seat?

How do different vendor preparation times affect shared dining?

What information makes waiting manageable?

4.4 Shared space

Is seating part of venue participation or independent of purchase?

Can guests locate suitable seating before ordering?

How do covered-space and accessibility needs alter seating choice?

Who manages conflict or scarcity in shared areas?

4.5 Multi-intent use

Can a visitor move between dining, events, leisure and nightlife without restarting arrival?

Which role changes require a new entitlement?

Which activities are genuinely optional?

Does the programme create breadth or participation pressure?

4.6 Inclusion and support

Can guests with dietary, mobility, sensory or family needs participate without disproportionate effort?

When is self-service insufficient?

Is help available at the point of difficulty?

Can a failed route recover without reducing the guest's status?

4.7 Responsibility

Which operator owns each participation object?

What remains the venue's responsibility in shared space?

Can guests recover without understanding the organisation first?

How should unresolved cross-operator problems be escalated?

9. Participation roles

A person may hold several roles during one visit.

Role 1 — General visitor

Enters and explores without a fixed transaction.

Role 2 — Diner

Selects, purchases and consumes food.

Role 3 — Drink purchaser

Uses a bar or coffee route.

Role 4 — Group organiser

Coordinates space, time, payment and participants.

Role 5 — Group participant

Uses a group entitlement without necessarily owning the booking record.

Role 6 — Event attendee

Participates through a free, registered or ticketed event state.

Role 7 — Leisure participant

Uses games, play or other activities.

Role 8 — Shopper or creative visitor

Engages with non-food operators, products or making.

Role 9 — Companion or caregiver

Supports another person's participation while retaining their own guest needs.

Role 10 — Prospective operator

Engages with Reffen as a platform or business opportunity rather than a guest product.

Stakeholder role and guest intent should not be collapsed.

13. Variety and decision burden

Large variety can support agency.

It can also create:

choice paralysis,

group delay,

repeated walking,

and uncertainty about suitability.

The solution is not necessarily to reduce variety.

It is to improve orientation through relevant decision variables.

20. Product waiting

Independent vendors may have different preparation times.

Product waiting should make clear:

that the order was accepted,

how readiness will be communicated,

where the guest may wait,

whether the guest can move away,

how an order is identified,

and what happens when preparation exceeds expectation.

The public evidence reviewed here does not establish a common Reffen-wide fulfilment system.

The correct waiting model may remain vendor-specific within a venue-wide legibility standard.

21. Asynchronous group dining

Orders may complete at different times.

This creates:

staggered return,

uneven food temperature,

seat guarding,

uncertain meal start,

and repeated movement.

The group meal may be socially shared while operationally asynchronous.

It acts as:

waiting infrastructure,

weather shelter where covered,

family infrastructure,

and social atmosphere.

It is a venue-level resource rather than a vendor-specific product unless a group reservation establishes otherwise.

The public evidence does not establish a live seating-allocation system.

24. Seat-first and food-first sequences

Different guests may need different orders of action.

Food first

Appropriate when the guest is mobile, confident and comfortable locating seating afterward.

Seat first

Appropriate for:

groups,

families,

guests with mobility needs,

people carrying belongings,

or weather-sensitive participation.

Split sequence

One participant holds or locates space while others purchase.

This can support the group.

It can also create social and fairness questions where capacity is scarce.

The venue should not assume one universal sequence.

25. Group participation

A reserved group area secures a shared base for a defined time.

It does not centralise food production.

Group participation may include:

staggered arrival,

organiser check-in,

card distribution,

independent vendor selection,

asynchronous collection,

shared seating,

optional beverage package,

event or activity participation,

and departure at different times.

The group organiser carries additional labour.

The venue can reduce that labour through:

clear entitlement,

participant instructions,

visible area identity,

payment readiness,

and one support owner.

32. Participation intensity

Participation is not binary.

A useful intensity scale is:

Level 0 — Presence

Enter, look, sit or accompany someone.

Level 1 — Passive participation

Watch music, observe craft or remain in shared atmosphere.

Level 2 — Transactional participation

Purchase food, drink or a product.

Level 3 — Social participation

Join a group, share seating or meet others.

Level 4 — Active leisure participation

Play a game, skate or use an activity.

Level 5 — Programmed participation

Join an event, workshop or structured activity.

Level 6 — Organising participation

Coordinate a group or event relationship.

Higher intensity is not inherently better.

The venue should not imply that quiet presence is incomplete participation.

40. Self-service sufficiency

Reffen's model depends heavily on guest movement, selection, ordering, collection and return.

Self-service can create:

variety,

informal pacing,

and direct operator contact.

It can become insufficient when the guest cannot:

stand in a queue,

carry products,

navigate while carrying,

leave a child or seat,

hear a collection signal,

or reconcile a failed order.

The human-assistance need should be discoverable without forcing the guest to perform incapacity publicly.

44. Participation failure states

44.1 Choice failure

The guest cannot identify an appropriate vendor or activity.

44.2 Vendor-state failure

The chosen operator is closed or unavailable.

44.3 Product-state failure

The desired item is sold out or cannot be prepared.

44.4 Dietary-confirmation failure

The vendor cannot provide a sufficiently reliable answer.

44.5 Payment failure

Card, cash, gift card, Reffen card or terminal route does not complete.

44.6 Fulfilment failure

An accepted order is late, incorrect, lost or unavailable.

44.7 Collection failure

The guest misses, cannot hear or cannot physically complete collection.

44.8 Seating failure

The guest has a product but no suitable place to consume it.

44.9 Group-coordination failure

Participants cannot locate one another, the area or the common plan.

44.10 Activity-capacity failure

Space or equipment is unavailable.

44.11 Event-access failure

Entry state, ticket or capacity prevents participation.

44.12 Accessibility failure

A published route becomes obstructed or unusable.

44.13 Weather failure

Conditions remove the intended activity or overwhelm covered capacity.

44.14 Responsibility failure

The guest is repeatedly redirected without a final owner.

45. Participation recovery hierarchy

Level 1 — Self-correction

Clear information lets the guest select another route.

Level 2 — Operator correction

The vendor, activity or event owner resolves the problem.

Level 3 — Venue routing

Venue staff identify and transfer the problem correctly.

Level 4 — Venue-level accommodation

Shared-space, access or safety needs require direct venue action.

Level 5 — Bounded failure

No appropriate route remains; the guest receives an honest explanation and any applicable refund, alternative or departure support.

Recovery quality is not measured only by whether the original outcome is restored.

It includes whether the guest remains informed, respected and able to choose.

46. Participation completion

Participation is complete when the relevant object has reached a clear outcome.

Product

Received, corrected, refunded or explicitly unresolved with an owner.

Group space

Used through the agreed period and closed without unresolved charges or belongings.

Event

Attended, declined, changed, refunded or otherwise resolved under the applicable terms.

Activity

Completed and equipment returned where required.

General visit

The visitor chooses to leave with no unresolved essential obligation.

The person may complete one participation object and continue into another.

49. New architecture directions

The following are research architecture only.

49.1 Participation-Object Router

49.2 Vendor Independence / Shared Standard

Preserve operator difference inside common minimum rules for information, payment state, access and recovery.

49.3 Asynchronous Group Dining

Coordinate shared social participation across independent queues and preparation times.

49.4 Seat-First Option

Support visitors who need a stable base before vendor selection.

49.5 Return-to-Group Wayfinding

Help guests relocate shared seating after dispersed purchasing.

49.6 Role-Change Continuity

Allow movement between visitor, diner, participant and attendee states without unnecessary restart.

49.7 Activity Capacity Object

Separate free price from equipment, space and time availability.

49.8 Participation Intensity Choice

Recognise presence, passive, transactional, social, active, programmed and organising states without ranking them.

49.9 Multi-Stock Waiting

Identify access, resource, product, service and exception waits with separate owners.

49.10 Cross-Operator Recovery

Preserve order and guest context when venue staff route to an independent operator.

49.11 Weather-State Participation

Connect conditions to shelter, movement, activity and capacity.

49.12 Dignified Outside-Food Exception

Provide a bounded route where safe participation cannot be supplied internally.

49.13 Accessible Fulfilment

Extend accessibility beyond entry into ordering, carrying, seating, events and toilets.

49.14 Participation Completion State

Make clear when a product, event, activity or group object has resolved.

52. Falsification programme

F01 — Intent-selection test

Hypothesis: visitors can choose a relevant participation route without understanding every operator.

Falsifier: guests cannot identify where or how to begin.

F02 — Vendor-comparison test

Hypothesis: variety can be narrowed through useful variables.

Falsifier: choice requires exhaustive physical search or social imitation.

F03 — Vendor-state test

Hypothesis: guests can identify whether a selected stall is operating.

Falsifier: availability becomes visible only at a closed counter.

F04 — Dietary-confirmation test

Hypothesis: filtered discovery can reach an accountable vendor answer.

Falsifier: labels are mistaken for safety or no sufficient answer exists.

F05 — Payment-to-order test

Hypothesis: payment and order states remain connected.

Falsifier: a guest cannot prove or recover a paid but missing order.

F06 — Product-wait test

Hypothesis: guests know how readiness will be communicated.

Falsifier: they must remain at the counter or risk losing the order.

F07 — Multi-vendor group test

Hypothesis: a group can purchase independently and reconvene effectively.

Falsifier: staggered fulfilment materially breaks shared dining.

F08 — Return-to-seat test

Hypothesis: guests can relocate their group after collection.

Falsifier: dense visual conditions make return difficult.

F09 — Seat-first test

Hypothesis: guests who need a base can find one before ordering.

Falsifier: seating information is available only after purchase.

F10 — Shared-space recovery test

Hypothesis: conflicts or scarcity have a visible venue-level owner.

Falsifier: every vendor disclaims responsibility and no common response exists.

F11 — Reffen-card test

Hypothesis: preloaded value supports independent group choice.

Falsifier: balance, acceptance or failure states create more organiser burden than they remove.

F12 — Free-activity test

Hypothesis: price, capacity and equipment are distinguishable.

Falsifier: “free” is interpreted as immediately available.

F13 — Equipment-custody test

Hypothesis: borrowing and return are understandable.

Falsifier: participants cannot find equipment or understand responsibility.

F14 — Event-role-change test

Hypothesis: a general visitor can become an event participant through a clear threshold.

Falsifier: access rules appear only at the event queue.

F15 — Optionality test

Hypothesis: guests can decline activities without losing core participation.

Falsifier: social or spatial design makes non-participation exclusionary.

F16 — Weather-participation test

Hypothesis: the venue can preserve a usable route under ordinary poor weather.

Falsifier: shelter scarcity collapses dining or access.

F17 — Crowd-state test

Hypothesis: busy conditions remain navigable and recoverable.

Falsifier: queues, paths and activities become indistinguishable.

F18 — Accessible-fulfilment test

Hypothesis: a guest can complete ordering, collection and seating with appropriate support.

Falsifier: accessibility ends at physical entry.

F19 — Outside-food exception test

Hypothesis: guests with necessary unmet food needs can reach a dignified decision.

Falsifier: the rule forces unsafe participation or exclusion.

F20 — Human-assistance test

Hypothesis: help appears where structured self-service fails.

Falsifier: the guest must publicly demonstrate inability or repeatedly seek unrelated staff.

F21 — Cross-operator recovery test

Hypothesis: the first help point can route context to the final owner.

Falsifier: the guest repeats the problem or loses proof between layers.

F22 — Participation-completion test

Hypothesis: each active object can reach a clear outcome.

Falsifier: unresolved payment, equipment, booking or event state follows the guest into departure.

F23 — Quiet-participation test

Hypothesis: presence or low-intensity use remains valid.

Falsifier: the venue experience implicitly requires purchasing, performance or social exposure.

F24 — Multi-intent transfer test

Hypothesis: intent changes are material enough to justify Multi-Intent Hospitality pressure.

Falsifier: most routes reduce to the same food-purchase journey with decorative extras.

F25 — Stable/variable transfer test

Hypothesis: stable venue rules support changing products and programmes.

Falsifier: variability repeatedly alters the participation contract itself.

F26 — Transfer-validity test

Hypothesis: restaurant-derived participation patterns remain useful after separating venue, vendor, group, event and activity objects.

Falsifier: the patterns cannot explain a visit assembled across independent operators.

61. Working conclusion

Reffen participation is neither one restaurant service nor an unstructured collection of independent actions.

It is a shared hospitality environment in which guests assemble a visit from several operators, resources and possible roles.

The strongest current abstraction is:

Guest Intent
→
Participation Object
→
Relevant Operator
→
Relevant Capacity
→
Choice or Entitlement
→
Action
→
Waiting and Shared Resources
→
Completion or Recovery
→
Optional Role Change

The venue's value comes partly from freedom, difference and spontaneous combination.

That freedom becomes usable hospitality only when the guest can understand where to act, what is available and who will respond when the route fails.

The core participation correction is:

≠

Self-Supported
