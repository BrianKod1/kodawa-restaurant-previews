1. Purpose of this file

This file studies how Reffen enables a person to enter, organise, pay for and complete participation.

For this institution, reservation.md means:

Booking / Access / Participation.

It cannot be reduced to table booking.

The public system contains several materially different routes:

general free entry,

individual walk-in food and drink participation,

group-area reservation,

free-event participation,

ticketed-event participation,

vendor-level ordering and payment,

venue gift-card payment,

group Reffen-card payment,

and human-assisted group coordination.

Each route creates a different combination of:

access,

capacity,

commitment,

payment,

entitlement,

responsibility,

failure,

and recovery.

The central problem is not:

Can the Guest Book Reffen?

It is:

Can the Guest Enter the Correct Participation Route,
Understand What It Secures,
and Reach the Correct Recovery Owner if It Fails?

2. Reservation boundary

Reservation begins when a visitor moves from general interest toward a route that can create access, space, payment or participation consequences.

That movement can occur through:

deciding to visit without booking,

contacting Reffen about a group,

reserving a Beer Garden table,

arranging a Werkstatt group area,

purchasing or using a Reffen card,

purchasing or using a gift card,

following an event-specific access route,

or placing an order with an independent food or drink operator.

Reservation does not have one universal completion state.

2.1 Venue-access state

The visitor reaches Reffen during an operating period and may enter the generally free site.

This state does not establish:

a table,

a vendor place,

a product,

an event place,

covered seating,

or a short queue.

2.2 Group-space state

The organiser receives a confirmed area or table for a defined group, place and time.

This state does not automatically establish:

food,

drinks,

table service,

vendor priority,

event admission,

or indefinite use of the space.

2.3 Event-access state

The participant enters a free, first-come, reservable or ticketed event route.

The entitlement depends on event-specific terms.

2.4 Vendor-transaction state

The guest places and pays for an order with a food stall, bar or coffee venue.

This state creates a product-level entitlement.

It does not reserve shared seating.

2.5 Participation-complete state

The visitor has received the relevant product, space, event access or other promised participation.

This state may be reached through several separate operators during one visit.

The governing boundary is:

Venue Access

≠

Space Entitlement

≠

Event Entitlement

≠

Product Entitlement

3. Primary research question

How should a multi-operator hospitality venue help guests understand and complete distinct walk-in, group, event and purchase routes when each route binds different capacity, payment, entitlement and responsibility objects?

4. Secondary research questions

4.1 General access

What does free entry permit?

What remains unreserved after the visitor enters?

Can a venue remain open while the guest's intended participation route is unavailable?

How should the system communicate crowd, weather, seating and vendor constraints?

4.2 Group booking

Which group sizes can reserve which spaces?

Which days and times are available?

What does the reservation secure?

What remains self-service?

Which payment and beverage options can be added?

What are the modification, cancellation and failure consequences?

4.3 Events

Which events are included in general free access?

Which events require separate tickets or registration?

Does a ticket secure only event entry or other venue entitlements?

Who owns event-level changes, cancellations and refunds?

4.4 Vendor transactions

Does the guest transact with Reffen, a vendor or shared payment infrastructure?

Who owns order accuracy and fulfilment?

Who owns dietary confirmation?

Who recovers failed payment, incorrect product or sold-out inventory?

4.5 Shared capacity

Can site entry remain open when seating or covered areas are full?

Does a vendor queue consume venue capacity or vendor capacity?

Can one live status represent the full place?

Which capacity states require advance visibility?

4.6 Recovery

Can the guest find help without knowing the operating structure?

When should venue staff route rather than own a problem?

What happens when responsibility is disputed?

Can recovery preserve guest dignity without creating operator ambiguity?

9. General walk-in access

The public system gives walk-in participation structural priority.

The general visitor does not need to reserve the venue before arrival.

This reduces:

booking friction,

advance financial commitment,

schedule rigidity,

and the need to predict a precise product choice.

It does not remove:

travel commitment,

queue exposure,

weather exposure,

seating uncertainty,

vendor availability uncertainty,

product-stock uncertainty,

or event-capacity uncertainty.

The accurate walk-in promise is not:

Everything will be available when you arrive.

It is closer to:

You can generally enter and discover available participation without making a prior venue reservation.

Reservation-Free

≠

Constraint-Free

10. Free-entry architecture

Free entry creates a venue-access entitlement only if the site is operating and no event-specific restriction applies.

It should not be interpreted as:

free food,

free drinks,

guaranteed seating,

guaranteed event admission,

reserved space,

or universal access to every connected environment.

The core entitlement is:

Permission to Enter the General Venue

subject to:

current operating state,

site rules,

safety conditions,

capacity conditions,

and event-specific boundaries.

Free entry lowers financial commitment.

It does not eliminate consequence-bearing uncertainty.

11. Group-booking architecture

The dedicated group page creates a human-assisted booking route.

The organiser is invited to contact Reffen by email or telephone.

This is appropriate because the request can vary by:

group size,

occasion,

preferred area,

time,

shelter requirement,

payment arrangement,

beverage service,

event conflict,

and other special conditions.

The group route is not presented as a generic self-service table widget.

This supports the Candidate pattern:

Human Assistance Where Structured Self-Service Is Insufficient.

It does not establish response time, accuracy or successful resolution.

16. Reffen-card boundary

The dedicated group page publishes suggested card amounts:

150 DKK, described as a main-course example;

220 DKK, described as a main course and beer example;

250 DKK, described as a main course and wine or cocktail example.

These figures are planning examples.

or universal product entitlements.

Each vendor controls its current offer and price unless another agreement states otherwise.

The design requirement is:

Explain stored value as spending capacity, not as a promise that a named combination will always be available for that amount.

18. Standard payment architecture

The practical-information page states that all bars and food stalls accept:

payment cards,

19. Vendor-transaction architecture

A vendor transaction contains at least five states.

State 1 — Selection

The guest selects a specific operator and product.

State 2 — Qualification

The guest confirms price, availability and any relevant dietary or preparation need.

State 3 — Payment

The guest provides an accepted payment method.

State 4 — Fulfilment waiting

The vendor prepares or retrieves the product.

State 5 — Collection

The guest receives the product and can identify any mismatch.

The transaction should preserve:

vendor identity,

order identity,

payment state,

fulfilment state,

and recovery route.

Shared venue identity should not erase the operator responsible for fulfilment.

20. Self-service boundary

coordinating a large group,

locating the table again,

mobility,

supervision of children,

and simultaneous vendor completion times.

≠

32. Commitment architecture

Reffen distributes commitment across several layers.

Layer 1 — No advance financial commitment

General free-entry walk-in visitor.

Layer 2 — Travel and time commitment

The visitor decides to reach Refshaleøen without securing product or seating.

Layer 3 — Space commitment

A group reserves an area for a defined time.

Layer 4 — Stored-value commitment

A gift card or group Reffen card is purchased or loaded.

Layer 5 — Product commitment

A vendor order is placed and paid.

An event registration or ticket creates date-specific participation.

The system should expose the consequence attached to each layer.

34. Failure-state architecture

34.1 Venue is closed or hours are unclear

Failure: the visitor cannot establish whether entry is possible.

Needed recovery: reconciled current-state information and direct confirmation.

34.2 Site is open but preferred vendor is closed

Failure: intended product route is unavailable.

Needed recovery: current vendor state and relevant alternatives.

34.3 Preferred product is sold out

Failure: vendor is operating but cannot fulfil the item.

Needed recovery: vendor-level substitution, refund or cancellation.

34.4 No suitable seating

Failure: entry and food purchase succeed but dining participation is impaired.

Needed recovery: visible seating alternatives, waiting guidance or take-away choice.

34.5 Covered area is full

Failure: weather-compatible capacity is unavailable.

Needed recovery: honest state communication and alternative route.

34.6 Group reservation cannot be located

Failure: space entitlement cannot be verified at arrival.

Needed recovery: named booking record, contact owner and provisional holding process.

34.7 Group size changes

Failure: the booked area may no longer fit the party.

Needed recovery: explicit modification route and capacity consequence.

34.8 Reffen card fails

Failure: prepaid spending value cannot be used.

Needed recovery: balance verification, alternative payment and issuer escalation.

34.9 Standard payment fails

Failure: vendor cannot complete the transaction.

Needed recovery: identify whether failure is card, cash, terminal, shared infrastructure or operator-specific.

34.10 Dietary need cannot be confirmed

Failure: possible product fit cannot become safe participation.

Needed recovery: another qualified vendor or permission for an appropriate exception.

34.11 Free event reaches capacity

Failure: free-entry expectation does not become event participation.

Needed recovery: capacity explanation, waiting or alternative programming.

34.12 Ticketed event is changed or cancelled

Failure: paid event entitlement cannot be delivered as confirmed.

Needed recovery: named ticket operator, notification and applicable refund or rescheduling route.

34.13 Crowd blocks accessible route

Failure: formal accessibility features do not produce usable movement.

Needed recovery: staff assistance, alternate route or crowd-management intervention.

34.14 Responsibility is disputed

Failure: venue and vendor each direct the guest elsewhere.

Needed recovery: no-wrong-door routing with documented transfer and final owner.

44. Provisional transfer findings

44.1 Reservation transfers as participation-object selection

The relevant question is not always whether the guest has booked.

It is what the guest has secured.

44.2 Walk-in can be primary and still need capacity proof

Live entry does not reveal seating, vendor, product or shelter availability.

44.3 One visit can contain several commitments

Group space, stored value, vendor products and event entry can be bound separately.

44.4 Human support can coordinate complexity without replacing self-service

The venue can retain independent selection and collection while using human coordination for groups and exceptions.

44.5 Responsibility routing is a hospitality function

The guest's need for a correct first response exists even when another operator owns final resolution.

44.6 Rule consistency is consequence-bearing

Different published booking windows can change whether a group believes participation is possible.

44.7 Stored value is not product inventory

Reffen cards and gift cards support payment flexibility but do not secure a dish, vendor or seat.

44.8 No transfer validation yet

One food-hall or venue case cannot establish cross-venue recurrence or validate restaurant-derived patterns.

46. Falsification programme

F01 — Free-entry comprehension

Hypothesis: visitors understand that free entry does not secure food, seating or event access.

Falsifier: users repeatedly infer broader entitlements.

F02 — Walk-in usability

Hypothesis: walk-in remains a first-class route during ordinary operating conditions.

Falsifier: guests require undocumented advance coordination for routine participation.

F03 — Group entitlement

Hypothesis: organisers can identify exactly what a reservation secures.

Falsifier: confirmation leaves food, service, space or time ambiguous.

F04 — Group-rule consistency

Hypothesis: direct coordination resolves the public time-window discrepancy.

Falsifier: contradictory rules persist through confirmation.

F05 — Large-group route

Hypothesis: Reffen can explain participation for groups above 100 where the page advertises up to 150.

Falsifier: no operable route exists or the published range is inaccurate.

F06 — Reffen-card comprehension

Hypothesis: organisers understand that suggested amounts are stored-value examples.

Falsifier: users interpret them as fixed meal packages or guaranteed prices.

F07 — Vendor payment ownership

Hypothesis: a guest can identify who owns an incorrect or failed purchase.

Falsifier: venue and vendor routes produce repeated transfers or refusal.

F08 — Gift-card recovery

Hypothesis: failed or disputed gift-card value has a legible recovery owner.

Falsifier: the guest cannot identify issuer, balance or accepting-party responsibility.

F09 — Event-access clarity

Hypothesis: event pages distinguish free, registered and ticketed participation.

Falsifier: guests arrive without the required entitlement or pay unnecessarily.

F10 — Capacity separation

Hypothesis: visitors can distinguish site access from seat, vendor and event availability.

Falsifier: venue-open information is routinely interpreted as complete capacity proof.

F11 — Dietary handoff

Hypothesis: venue-level discovery can reach an accountable vendor answer.

Falsifier: the guest cannot obtain or retain a clear confirmation.

F12 — Accessibility under crowd pressure

Hypothesis: staff and alternate routes preserve usable participation during bottlenecks.

Falsifier: published features cannot be used during realistic peak conditions.

F13 — No-wrong-door recovery

Hypothesis: venue staff can route a vendor or event failure without abandoning the guest.

Falsifier: the guest must independently locate the responsible operator.

F14 — Modification clarity

Hypothesis: group changes receive explicit consequence and reconfirmation.

Falsifier: changed details remain distributed across messages or staff memory.

F15 — Mobile interruption

Hypothesis: a group or event route can resume after interruption without losing state.

Falsifier: the visitor must restart or repeat information.

F16 — Source freshness

Hypothesis: consequence-bearing rules can be reconciled to one current authority.

Falsifier: conflicting current pages remain equally authoritative.

F17 — Data-boundary clarity

Hypothesis: visitors can understand when another operator receives their transaction or event data.

Falsifier: shared branding obscures the responsible data relationship.

F18 — Transfer validity

Hypothesis: restaurant-derived participation patterns can describe a distributed venue when their objects are separated.

Falsifier: the patterns collapse distinct entry, space, event, product and payment states into one reservation concept.
