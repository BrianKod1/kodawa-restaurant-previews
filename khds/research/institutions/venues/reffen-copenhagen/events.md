1. Purpose of this file

This file studies events at Reffen as temporary hospitality systems operating inside a continuing multi-operator venue.

Reffen is not only a place where events happen.

Programming is part of how the institution describes itself.

The official event surface includes or describes:

concerts,

DJ programmes,

food and drink events,

guided tastings,

creative workshops,

craft, flea and vinyl markets,

children's programmes,

street-art activity,

seasonal celebrations,

social rides,

nightlife,

private events,

and ticketed activity in Werkstatt.

These formats do not create one common participation route.

Some can be encountered through ordinary free venue entry.

Some require a ticket.

Some are free but capacity-bounded.

Some create a separate participant role.

Some allow open visitor access while charging exhibitors, booth holders or other contributors.

This file asks what the guest must understand when a stable venue becomes a changing event state.

2. Event boundary

For this case, an event is:

A time-bounded programme that changes what guests may encounter, do, access or expect within all or part of Reffen.

An event can alter:

purpose,

timing,

access,

entitlement,

capacity,

crowd density,

sound,

lighting,

food and drink demand,

age relevance,

This file does not treat every lively moment as an event.

A crowded ordinary afternoon, spontaneous social interaction or background playlist may shape atmosphere without constituting a separately governed programme.

3. Primary research question

How should a hospitality venue communicate and govern temporary event states when ordinary dining, drinking, leisure and public access continue around them?

4. Secondary research questions

6. Verified public event facts

Current official evidence establishes that:

Reffen hosts seasonal events involving music, food, art and culture.

The events page describes concerts, DJs, children's summer activity, street art, flea markets, vinyl markets and seasonal celebrations.

General admission to Reffen is described as free, including many concerts and similar events.

Reffen states that some events in Werkstatt may be ticketed.

Individual event pages can publish a title, date, time, location, price, programme, food relationship and external ticket or information link.

A current Wine Club example requires a paid ticket through an external ticket route and includes a defined tasting package.

A current creative-workshop example is described as free and guided at Reffen's coffee bar.

A current craft-market example presents open visitor participation while linking to a separate booth-purchase route.

A current social-ride example publishes check-in, departure, return, route type, distance, speed, group definitions and maximum participants.

A current multi-day seasonal celebration publishes a preliminary programme across several locations and describes food stalls and bars continuing to operate.

Werkstatt presents itself through a separate website, email routes and event/nightlife identity while remaining physically at Reffen.

Reffen's group page states that Werkstatt group reservations can be displaced by private or ticketed events.

Reffen's public accessibility information acknowledges busy-day bottlenecks and identifies accessible physical infrastructure and staff-help claims.

These facts establish several event types and participation states.

They do not establish consistent delivery, accessibility or guest outcome.

7. Events as temporary operating states

An event changes the venue without permanently replacing it.

The relevant model is:

Base Venue State
→
Scheduled Event Overlay
→
Temporary Rules and Conditions
→
Event Completion
→
Return to Base or Next State

The event overlay may affect:

one small area,

one operator,

one route,

one audience,

several zones,

or the full venue identity for a period.

The event is therefore a temporal state, not merely a content item.

8. Event architecture at Reffen

The public examples support at least seven event modes.

8.1 Open ambient programme

Music, DJs or seasonal activity may be encountered through ordinary venue use without a separate commitment.

8.2 Open scheduled event

A guest deliberately visits for a published programme but does not need a paid entitlement.

8.3 Free guided participation

A workshop or facilitated activity is free but may depend on space, materials, timing and facilitator capacity.

8.4 Capped active participation

A route such as a social ride may be free or separately managed while still having a hard participant limit and preparation requirements.

8.5 Paid event entitlement

A tasting or similar event creates a ticketed product with a defined package, time and location.

8.6 Open visitor / paid contributor event

A market may be open to visitors while exhibitors or booth holders need a separate commercial entitlement.

8.7 Werkstatt or private event

An indoor nightlife, private, conference or ticketed event may use a more controlled threshold and a different operating owner.

These modes must not be collapsed into a single event = free assumption.

9. Venue entry and event access

Reffen's practical information describes general venue entry as free and says this includes many concerts and similar events.

It also explicitly allows for ticketed Werkstatt events.

Current event examples additionally show paid participation outside a generic nightlife framing, including a priced guided tasting.

The correct model is:

Venue Entry State

≠

Event Entitlement State

A guest can be:

admitted to Reffen but not entitled to a paid event,

admitted to the venue and able to encounter an open programme,

registered for a capped activity,

ticketed for one bounded product,

or commercially registered as a contributor rather than attending as a visitor.

10. Free entry is not unlimited event access

“Free” can refer to several different objects:

no general gate fee,

no event ticket price,

no participant fee,

no spectator fee,

It does not necessarily mean:

unlimited capacity,

guaranteed admission,

no registration,

all materials included,

all food and drinks included,

no age or safety conditions,

12. Event roles

A person at Reffen may occupy one or more event roles.

12.1 Incidental visitor

The person visits for the venue and encounters an event without planning around it.

12.2 Intentional attendee

The person chooses Reffen because of a programme.

12.3 Spectator

The person watches without materially joining the activity.

12.4 Active participant

The person makes, rides, tastes, dances, plays or otherwise performs a defined action.

12.5 Ticket holder

The person has a financial or registered entitlement.

12.6 Diner or drink purchaser

The person uses the food and bar system before, during or after the event.

12.7 Parent, guardian or companion

The person supports another participant and may have different access needs.

12.8 Booth holder or seller

The person participates commercially rather than only as a guest.

12.9 Performer, facilitator or collaborator

The person delivers part of the programme.

12.10 Organiser

The person or organisation governs programme, capacity, terms or recovery.

Role must not be inferred from presence alone.

14. Multi-intent events

Events can attract materially different intents:

hear music,

browse a market,

join a ride,

attend nightlife,

These are not merely different products.

Intent can change:

One case does not change its maturity.

22. Open visitor / paid contributor separation

The current Craft Market page invites visitors to explore and separately links to buying a booth.

This creates two materially different participation objects:

The booth holder may need:

25. Ability and compatibility

Active events require more than interest.

A participant may need:

physical capacity,

relevant skill,

appropriate equipment,

age eligibility,

language comprehension,

risk understanding,

and enough time to complete the route.

The Social Ride example improves compatibility confidence by publishing distance and expected speed for different groups.

requires

27. Zone-specific thresholds

Different event zones can have different thresholds.

Open square

The guest may move in and out without a separate check.

Bar or coffee-bar activity

The event may share a commercial service area with ordinary customers.

Workshop zone

Material and facilitator capacity may form the threshold.

Social-ride check-in

The threshold may include timing, participant list, group selection and equipment readiness.

Ticketed tasting

The threshold may include ticket confirmation and punctual arrival.

Werkstatt

The threshold may include a separate door, event check, age or security conditions where applicable.

Public evidence does not establish every live threshold.

28. Reffen / Werkstatt relationship

Reffen presents Werkstatt as a venue within its wider environment.

Werkstatt also maintains a separate public identity, website and contact routes.

The relationship is both connected and differentiated.

≠

Shared Address and Place Identity

≠

This matters because:

29. Ordinary use during an event

An event may coexist with ordinary venue use.

The current Craft Market page explicitly says food stalls and bars remain open.

The current seasonal-celebration page also publishes food-stall and bar continuity.

This creates a dual state:

Ordinary Venue Operations

+

Temporary Event Programme

An ordinary guest may:

dine without attending deliberately,

pass through an event area,

become an incidental spectator,

join part of the programme,

or avoid it.

The interface should clarify whether the event changes ordinary access, queues, seating or sound conditions.

30. Incidental event exposure

Not every guest chooses the event.

A person may arrive for food and encounter:

amplified music,

dense crowds,

a market occupying normal circulation space,

a children's programme,

a queue or check-in point,

or a private area closure.

The event can affect that guest even without event intent.

≠

This expands the event communication audience beyond registered participants.

31. Dining continuity

Events can change the food and drink journey through:

increased demand,

altered operating hours,

venue-zone closures,

special menus or promotions,

event-specific inclusions,

separate bar service,

group timing,

and late-evening transitions.

The event page should distinguish:

food included in the event entitlement,

food available for separate purchase,

food operating only before or after the programme,

and food not available in the event zone.

The Wine Club example does this comparatively well by stating what the ticket includes and indicating that food stalls can be explored afterward.

32. Event time architecture

Event time is not one timestamp.

It can include:

publication time,

ticket-release time,

registration deadline,

recommended arrival,

check-in,

doors,

start,

individual programme segments,

intermission,

end,

expected return,

venue closing,

and transport departure.

The Social Ride example distinguishes check-in, rollout and approximate return.

The Wine Club example publishes start and end.

The seasonal celebration distributes activity across several days and zones.

The correct object is a timeline, not a date label.

33. Arrival tolerance

Different event types have different late-arrival consequences.

Ambient programme

Late arrival may have little effect.

Market

The guest loses browsing time but may still participate.

Workshop

Late arrival may interrupt guidance or exceed material capacity.

Tasting

Late arrival may miss part of a sequenced product.

Social ride

Arrival after rollout may eliminate participation entirely.

Ticketed nightlife

Door, re-entry and last-entry rules may govern.

The event page should publish time consequences proportional to the event's sequence dependency.

44. Event waiting

Events can activate the existing KHDS waiting taxonomy.

Access Waiting

Waiting for ticket release, registration, doors or event-zone entry.

Resource Waiting

Waiting for space, materials, equipment, seating, toilets, shelter or transport.

Product Waiting

Waiting for included tasting items, food, drinks or purchased goods.

Service Waiting

Waiting for check-in, facilitation, performer start or organiser assistance.

Exception Waiting

Waiting for ticket recovery, cancellation decision, accessibility support, refund or re-routing.

No new formal waiting category is required.

The event case shows time-bounded consequences across the existing categories.

45. Event failure states

45.1 Stale listing

The guest relies on a page that no longer reflects the live event state.

45.2 Date or time conflict

Different official or partner sources publish different details.

45.3 Wrong event zone

The guest reaches Reffen but cannot find the programme.

45.4 False free-access inference

The guest interprets free venue entry as entitlement to a paid or bounded event.

45.5 Hidden capacity

A free activity is full although the event page did not disclose a participation rule.

45.6 Ticket handoff failure

The external checkout, confirmation or ticket cannot be completed or retrieved.

45.7 Role confusion

A visitor follows a booth-holder or participant route, or a contributor cannot find their operational instructions.

45.8 Late arrival

The guest reaches the event after a sequence-dependent threshold.

45.9 Weather disruption

Outdoor conditions alter, delay or cancel the programme.

45.10 Accessibility mismatch

The venue is reachable but the specific activity cannot be used as expected.

45.11 Sensory mismatch

Sound, lighting or crowd state materially differs from the guest's reasonable expectation.

45.12 Food continuity failure

Published nearby food service is closed, overloaded or not accessible during the event.

45.13 Participant compatibility failure

Ability, pace, equipment or age conditions are discovered too late.

45.14 Private-event collision

A group or ordinary visitor expects a space that has become unavailable through another event state.

45.15 Cancellation ambiguity

The event is cancelled but the governing update and recovery route are unclear.

45.16 Responsibility bounce

Reffen, Werkstatt, organiser, partner and ticket platform each direct the guest elsewhere.

46. Recovery hierarchy

A useful event-recovery sequence is:

Detect Changed or Failed State
→
Identify Event and Guest Role
→
Identify Affected Entitlement
→
Identify Current Authority
→
Publish Immediate Consequence
→
Offer Refund / Rebooking / Re-route / Support
→
Preserve Ordinary Venue Participation where possible

Recovery should answer:

Is the venue still open?

Is the event happening?

Is the location changed?

59. Falsification programme

59.1 Event-overlay model

Hypothesis: Event programming materially changes ordinary venue conditions while core operations continue.

Could be falsified if: Events are fully isolated and have no meaningful effect on ordinary guests or operations.

Needed evidence: Zone observation, operations mapping and ordinary-visitor interviews.

59.2 Access / entitlement separation

Hypothesis: Guests need explicit distinction between free venue entry and event-specific access.

Could be falsified if: Current language produces no confusion across all event types.

Needed evidence: Comprehension testing and support logs.

59.3 Role-specific contracts

Hypothesis: Visitor, participant and contributor routes require different information and recovery.

Could be falsified if: One common route serves all roles without loss or confusion.

Needed evidence: Journey comparison and event-operations records.

59.4 Programme freshness

Hypothesis: Explicit event state and update time materially improve appropriate commitment.

Could be falsified if: Guests already receive current authoritative information through another reliable surface.

Needed evidence: Source audit, analytics and missed-event reports.

59.5 Preliminary programme confidence

Hypothesis: Separating fixed and open details reduces uncertainty without suppressing early discovery.

Could be falsified if: Preliminary publication produces more harmful expectation mismatch than benefit.

Needed evidence: Update history and guest-expectation testing.

59.6 Event-zone arrival

Hypothesis: Guests experience meaningful orientation failure after reaching Reffen.

Could be falsified if: Zones and check-ins are immediately legible on site.

Needed evidence: First-visit walkthroughs and arrival observation.

59.7 Incidental exposure

Hypothesis: Events materially affect guests who did not intend to attend.

Could be falsified if: Event zones and effects remain fully optional and avoidable.

Needed evidence: Ordinary-visitor observation across programme states.

59.8 Active-event compatibility

Hypothesis: Pace, distance, equipment and ability information materially improve suitable self-selection.

Could be falsified if: Organiser screening or live guidance already resolves compatibility without pre-arrival burden.

Needed evidence: Participant outcomes, withdrawals and organiser interviews.

59.9 Event accessibility

Hypothesis: Event-specific access information is necessary beyond base-venue accessibility.

Could be falsified if: All programmes inherit and preserve the same accessible route and conditions.

Needed evidence: Accessibility audits across event types.

59.10 External ticket handoff

Hypothesis: Cross-surface authority and recovery create material guest friction.

Could be falsified if: The external platform provides a seamless, trusted and complete route.

Needed evidence: Funnel analytics, failed-payment data and ticket-support records.

59.11 Event responsibility router

Hypothesis: Object-based routing reduces resolution effort across collaborators.

Could be falsified if: One common event desk already owns complete recovery.

Needed evidence: Complaint and escalation mapping.

59.12 Programme-end / completion separation

Hypothesis: Departure, property and group-reassembly needs remain material after programme end.

Could be falsified if: Guests complete these transitions without meaningful friction.

Needed evidence: Egress observation and post-event interviews.
