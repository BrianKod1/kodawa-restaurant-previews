1. Purpose of this file

This file studies how Reffen becomes discoverable, desirable, understandable and worth visiting before a guest begins travel, joins a queue, makes a vendor transaction or enters an event.

Discovery is not treated as traffic acquisition alone.

For KHDS, venue discovery includes the period in which a potential visitor attempts to answer:

What kind of place is Reffen?

Where is it?

Is it operating in the current season?

What is open today?

Is the visit mainly for food, drinks, events, leisure, family activity or nightlife?

Can I enter without booking?

Does my intended activity require a reservation or ticket?

Which food vendors or dietary routes may suit me?

How do I get there and return?

What changes with weather, crowd level, vendor state or event state?

Can the venue support my access needs?

Should I continue toward a visit, group enquiry or event-specific action?

Restaurant discovery can often move from appetite to menu to reservation.

Reffen requires a different qualification process.

The visitor may be evaluating:

a street-food market,

a waterfront social destination,

an independent-vendor collection,

a cultural programme,

a family environment,

a group meeting place,

a creative marketplace,

or a nightlife venue.

The central discovery question is therefore:

How does Reffen help a visitor recognise the right version of the venue for their intent while making season, access, operator and availability boundaries sufficiently clear?

2. Discovery boundary

Discovery begins before a visitor reaches the official website.

It can begin through:

search,

maps,

tourism information,

event listings,

a specific food stall,

a specific cuisine,

social media,

images of the waterfront,

music or nightlife promotion,

family-activity research,

word of mouth,

group planning,

or stories about Refshaleøen and Copenhagen's harbourfront.

Discovery ends when the visitor has formed a sufficiently specific intent to:

travel to Reffen as a walk-in visitor,

choose a likely food or activity route,

inspect same-day conditions,

contact Reffen about a group,

reserve a group area,

enter an event-specific route,

or decide that the venue is not currently appropriate.

It does not necessarily end when the visitor opens:

the food directory,

an event listing,

a group-booking page,

a map,

or an external Werkstatt page.

Those surfaces may still be helping the visitor determine what kind of visit is possible.

3. Primary research question

How does a seasonal, multi-operator hospitality venue convert broad interest into an appropriate visit intention without implying that every vendor, product, seat, activity or event is continuously available?

4. Secondary research questions

4.1 Recognition

Can a first-time visitor immediately recognise Reffen as a street-food market?

Can the same visitor understand that Reffen also supports cultural, social, family and nightlife use?

Does the phrase “Copenhagen Street Food” reduce category uncertainty?

Does the broader cultural framing create useful possibility or category ambiguity?

4.2 Intent

Does the interface help visitors identify what they want to do?

Are food, bars, events, exploration and practical information presented as distinct routes?

Does an event visitor receive different preparation from a casual diner?

Can group organisers distinguish a table reservation from a private event or ticketed programme?

4.3 Current state

Can the visitor identify the current season?

Can the visitor identify today's venue, food, bar and coffee hours?

Are event dates and live availability distinguishable from evergreen descriptions?

What happens when official pages disagree?

Can the visitor determine whether a listed vendor is operating now?

4.4 Fit

Can guests identify relevant dietary possibilities before travel?

Can guests with mobility needs identify usable routes and likely crowd conditions?

Can families understand which activities are suitable and current?

Can guests understand the weather exposure and covered alternatives?

4.5 Trust

Which claims are supported through observable venue mechanisms?

Which claims remain brand promises?

Are independent vendor stories clearly attributed?

Does the interface distinguish curated participation from verified operator performance?

4.6 Progression

Is “visit” the correct next action for every visitor?

When should the visitor move to food discovery, events, practical information or group contact?

Does discovery produce the information needed for pre-arrival preparation?

Can a visitor leave the discovery process with appropriate uncertainty rather than false certainty?

The current public navigation gives high visibility to:

This creates a route-oriented discovery system rather than a single linear story.

Stage 1 — Category cue

The Reffen and Copenhagen Street Food identity establishes food as the most recognisable entry category.

Function: reduce first-visit category uncertainty.

Pressure: food should remain primary without concealing the venue's other intents.

Stage 2 — Current-state cue

Opening hours are repeatedly surfaced across official pages.

Function: support immediate visit feasibility.

Pressure: repeated hours can harm confidence if repeated values disagree.

Stage 3 — Food exploration

The official directory lets visitors search stalls and use dietary-category filters.

Function: convert general appetite into possible vendor routes.

Pressure: directory presence does not prove same-day opening, stock or dietary safety.

Stage 4 — Event exploration

The event calendar presents dated cultural and social activity.

Function: convert a general venue visit into a time-specific intent.

Pressure: event-specific access, capacity and operator boundaries may require separate verification.

Stage 5 — Place exploration

The Explore surface presents leisure activities, creative businesses and stories.

Function: demonstrate that a visit can extend beyond purchasing food.

Pressure: current, seasonal and archived activities can appear within a common exploratory frame.

Stage 6 — Practical qualification

Practical information covers transport, hours, food, groups, accessibility and selected policies.

Function: prepare an intended visit.

Pressure: a broad FAQ can distribute consequence-bearing facts across expandable sections.

Stage 7 — Group escalation

The group page supports enquiries and area reservations for larger parties.

Function: move complex participation toward human coordination.

Pressure: the group page and general FAQ currently publish different booking ranges and time windows.

Stage 8 — Ongoing relationship

Stories, newsletters, social accounts and event updates extend discovery over time.

Function: make a seasonal venue repeatedly discoverable.

Pressure: the English-language site notes that the newsletter is in Danish, creating a language boundary for some visitors.

9. Discovery audiences

Reffen does not have one discovery audience.

At least eleven intent states are visible.

Audience 1 — The casual diner

The visitor wants food without advance booking.

Need: current hours, food range, transport and basic arrival confidence.

Audience 2 — The cuisine-led visitor

The visitor is looking for a particular cuisine, dish or dietary possibility.

Need: searchable vendor information and a clear freshness boundary.

Audience 3 — The social group

The visitor wants several people to sit together while choosing food independently.

Need: group-size rules, table entitlement, time window, self-service explanation and payment options.

Audience 4 — The event-led visitor

The visitor discovers Reffen through a concert, workshop, market, festival or tasting.

Need: event date, start time, location, access rule, capacity state and relationship to general venue hours.

Audience 5 — The family visitor

The visitor wants food plus age-appropriate activities.

Need: current activity state, safety, accessibility, toilets, shelter and duration guidance.

Audience 6 — The tourist

The visitor may know Copenhagen but not Refshaleøen.

Need: category recognition, route comparison, operating season and return transport confidence.

Audience 7 — The local repeat visitor

The visitor already understands the venue and wants to know what changed.

Need: current events, new vendors, seasonal state and live differences.

Audience 8 — The accessibility planner

The visitor needs to determine whether the site and intended route are usable.

Need: surface information, ramps, toilets, crowd-state warning, parking boundary and human-assistance route.

Audience 9 — The weather-sensitive visitor

The visitor needs to understand whether rain, cold, heat or wind changes the visit.

Need: current conditions and realistic covered-space options.

Audience 10 — The prospective entrepreneur

The visitor wants to understand Reffen as a platform for a stall, truck or creative business.

Need: selection criteria, shared infrastructure, financial structure and application state.

Audience 11 — The creative or leisure explorer

The visitor is interested in workshops, art, shopping, games, skating or other non-food activity.

Need: current availability, location, participation rule and relationship to venue hours.

10.4 Event search

Likely need: event access and general venue relationship.

10.5 Tourism and map discovery

The visitor enters through location, directions or destination content.

Likely need: category recognition and visit feasibility.

10.6 Social discovery

The visitor encounters food, crowds, music, harbour imagery or a specific operator.

Likely need: translation from visual promise to current practical state.

10.7 Group-planning discovery

The visitor enters through a celebration, school, workplace or large-party need.

Likely need: entitlement, size, time, cost and human contact.

10.8 Family-activity discovery

The visitor enters through play, camp or child-focused content.

Likely need: date, age fit, supervision, facilities and weather state.

10.9 Refshaleøen discovery

The visitor is exploring the wider harbour area.

Likely need: Reffen's role within the destination and the boundary between nearby operators.

10.10 Entrepreneur discovery

The visitor enters through the stall-application route.

Likely need: a stakeholder-specific interface rather than guest guidance.

11. Discovery jobs

Job 1 — Recognition

Identify Reffen as a visitable hospitality venue.

Job 2 — Intent formation

Determine whether the visit is led by food, sociality, events, family activity, exploration, group use or nightlife.

Job 3 — State qualification

Determine whether the relevant season, day, time, vendor, event and facility states support the intended visit.

Job 4 — Fit qualification

Assess dietary, mobility, group, age, weather and transport compatibility.

Job 5 — Difference understanding

Understand why the combination of independent operators, reused industrial place and cultural programming matters.

Job 6 — Responsibility orientation

Recognise that some promises and transactions belong to specific vendors, event operators or external systems.

Job 7 — Safe progression

Move toward walk-in travel, group contact, vendor inspection or event participation with appropriate confidence.

24. Multi-operator discovery boundary

Reffen's shared identity can create a reasonable guest inference that the venue coordinates the experience.

Public evidence also shows operator independence.

Discovery should therefore help the visitor distinguish:

venue information,

vendor information,

shared infrastructure,

event information,

and external-system information.

The visitor should not need a legal entity chart.

The visitor does need to know which information source can safely answer the next question.

The working discovery rule is:

Reveal responsibility at the point where it changes the guest's decision, preparation, entitlement or recovery route.

28. Discovery friction classification

28.1 Meaningful friction

Friction can be meaningful when it encourages:

exploration among independent vendors,

discovery of unfamiliar cuisines,

browsing of creative activity,

or movement through the venue as part of the experience.

28.2 Necessary friction

Friction can be necessary when the visitor must:

confirm a dietary requirement with a vendor,

distinguish a ticketed event,

contact the venue for a large group,

inspect weather or transport state,

or understand an external responsibility boundary.

28.3 Avoidable friction

Friction is avoidable when it results from:

conflicting hours,

conflicting group rules,

stale activity pages,

unclear current vendor state,

unclear free-versus-ticketed event access,

or unclear ownership of a consequential answer.

29. Priority KHDS patterns under pressure

29.1 Multi-Intent Hospitality — Candidate / Medium

Reffen provides a strong intent-diversity test across food, drink, groups, events, family use, leisure, creative activity and nightlife.

The case does not yet establish that every offer represents a distinct hospitality intent or that the routes are equally legible.

29.2 Walk-In as First-Class Participation — Candidate / High

Walk-in is the dominant general route.

The case tests whether walk-in participation remains usable when site access is open but vendor, table, weather or crowd capacity is constrained.

29.3 Layered Arrival — Candidate / Medium

Discovery must connect current season, transport, venue threshold, physical orientation and vendor selection.

The case can pressure layered arrival without assuming that complexity must become interface complexity.

29.4 Hospitality System Proof — Candidate / High

Public sources show operator selection and shared infrastructure.

The case tests whether those mechanisms are legible and relevant to guest decisions.

29.5 Capacity Proof — Candidate / Medium-High

Reffen separates entry, seating, circulation, vendor, stock, event and covered-space capacity.

The case tests whether capacity proof can remain useful when no single indicator describes the full visit.

29.6 Stable Participation / Variable Product — Candidate / Medium-High

Venue-level participation may remain stable while vendors, menus, stock, events, seasons and weather vary.

The case must establish which rules are genuinely stable.

29.7 Photography as Use-Case Evidence — Candidate / Medium-High

Discovery imagery must support several materially different uses of the same place.

29.8 Story Through Place — Candidate / High

Refshaleøen's industrial history and waterfront setting contribute to institutional meaning.

29.9 Story Through Hospitality Model — Candidate / High

Entrepreneur selection, shared infrastructure and independent delivery can function as narrative mechanisms.

29.10 Own the Guest Relationship — Partially Supported / Medium

The venue controls some discovery, group and gift-card routes while transactions and customer data may be distributed.

Public evidence cannot establish complete relationship ownership.

No maturity or confidence movement is made in this file.

31. Provisional discovery findings

Finding 1 — Food provides category recognition but not the complete institution

“Copenhagen Street Food” quickly orients first-time visitors.

Events, activities, groups, creative operators and nightlife expand the venue beyond that first category.

Finding 2 — Discovery must begin with intent before content depth

The visitor needs different information depending on whether the intended visit concerns food, a group, an event, family activity, accessibility or entrepreneurship.

Finding 3 — Season is an operating state

Season changes available products, programmes, weather exposure and likely guest intent.

Finding 4 — The venue directory is a possibility map

It supports narrowing and orientation.

It is not complete live availability or safety proof.

Finding 5 — Operator stories distribute protagonism

Reffen's story is carried by the venue, place and independent operators rather than one chef or one product.

Finding 6 — Multi-intent discovery requires route-specific next actions

A single generic “visit” action cannot safely complete every discovery journey.

Finding 7 — Responsibility becomes relevant before failure

The visitor needs to know where consequential confirmation belongs before travelling or committing.

Finding 8 — Repeated current-state content requires reconciliation

Repeated hours improve visibility only when they describe the same state consistently.

Finding 9 — Free entry lowers financial commitment but not every burden

Transport, weather, crowding, queueing, seating and product uncertainty can remain.

Finding 10 — Place is both atmosphere and wayfinding context

F01 — Category recognition

Falsifier: visitors repeatedly enter irrelevant surfaces or cannot identify the correct next action.

F03 — Current-state confidence

Hypothesis: repeated opening information supports visit feasibility.

Falsifier: official conflicts cause incorrect arrival or prevent confident planning.

F04 — Vendor discovery

Hypothesis: the directory helps guests narrow from broad food interest to possible vendor fit.

Falsifier: listed information is too stale or generic to affect vendor selection.

F05 — Dietary orientation

Hypothesis: filters plus vendor confirmation create a useful two-stage discovery route.

Falsifier: guests interpret filters as safety guarantees or cannot reach a responsible confirmation source.

F06 — Multi-intent hospitality

Hypothesis: the venue supports materially different guest intents.

Falsifier: the additional activities do not materially change preparation, participation or expected outcome.

F07 — Seasonal orientation

Hypothesis: the season page helps visitors identify which version of Reffen is operating.

Falsifier: seasonal information does not resolve current activity or venue state.

F08 — Group handoff

Hypothesis: complex group needs receive an appropriate human-assisted route.

Falsifier: contradictory rules or unclear entitlements persist after the handoff.

F09 — Accessibility discovery

Hypothesis: published facilities and crowd warnings improve visit preparation.

Falsifier: the information is insufficient to plan an end-to-end usable route.

F10 — Responsibility legibility

Hypothesis: the public architecture can route consequential questions without requiring organisational knowledge.

Falsifier: guests must infer or repeatedly transfer between venue, vendor, event and external operators.

F11 — Place story

Hypothesis: industrial reuse explains both visual identity and operating difference.

Falsifier: users perceive it only as decorative style with no effect on understanding.

F12 — Transfer validity

Hypothesis: restaurant-derived discovery patterns remain useful in a distributed venue.

Falsifier: they cannot account for intent diversity, operator boundaries and several simultaneous capacity states.
