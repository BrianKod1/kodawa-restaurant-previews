1. Purpose of this file

This file studies the period between deciding to visit Reffen and crossing the physical venue threshold.

For a general walk-in visitor, that period may last only a few minutes.

For a group organiser or event participant, it may begin days or weeks earlier.

Pre-arrival is not treated as administrative waiting time.

It is the stage in which a possible visit must become:

current,

reachable,

appropriately prepared,

compatible with the guest's needs,

aligned with the correct entitlement,

and recoverable if a material condition changes.

Reffen complicates pre-arrival because the public venue operates through:

seasons,

several daily operating clocks,

multiple transport routes,

outdoor and covered environments,

independent food operators,

variable vendor and product states,

free and ticketed events,

group reservations,

shared seating,

and external systems that Reffen does not control.

Pre-arrival must therefore answer two linked questions:

Can the Guest Reach Reffen?

and:

Will the Relevant Version of Reffen
Support the Guest's Intended Participation?

2. Pre-arrival boundary

Pre-arrival begins when a visitor forms a sufficiently specific intention to attend.

That can occur when the visitor:

decides to make a general walk-in visit,

chooses a likely food or activity route,

confirms a group area,

obtains a ticket or registration,

arranges a Reffen card or beverage package,

or commits to travel toward Refshaleøen.

Pre-arrival can also begin before formal commitment when a consequential question must be resolved first, including:

mobility,

dietary safety,

event access,

large-group capacity,

transport feasibility,

or weather compatibility.

Pre-arrival ends when the guest enters the live physical arrival system.

Depending on the route, that can occur:

at the site entrance,

at a group meeting point,

at a Werkstatt event threshold,

at a vendor queue,

or when venue staff begin receiving or redirecting the guest.

2.3 Working definition

For Case #23:

Pre-arrival is the period in which a visitor converts an intention, reservation or event entitlement into a current route, appropriate preparation and recoverable handoff into Reffen's live multi-operator environment.

3. Primary research question

How should a seasonal, open-entry and multi-operator venue prepare guests for a visit when transport, weather, crowd, vendor, seating and event states can change independently?

4. Secondary research questions

4.1 Current-state readiness

What should the visitor recheck on the day of arrival?

Which source governs when official pages disagree?

Can venue, food, bar, coffee and event states be understood separately?

How should temporary closures or changes be communicated?

4.2 Route readiness

Which transport route best fits the guest's time, mobility, party and return plan?

Which route components are controlled by external operators?

What must be saved for offline or low-connectivity use?

How should return travel be considered before arrival?

4.3 Weather readiness

What changes when conditions are wet, cold, windy, hot or unusually sunny?

Are covered areas sufficient for the expected crowd?

Which activities or routes are weather-dependent?

Can weather preparation remain practical rather than alarmist?

4.4 Personal readiness

What should a visitor with mobility, dietary, sensory or family requirements confirm?

What can be prepared at venue level?

What must be confirmed with a vendor or event operator?

Which accommodations cannot be inferred from public pages?

4.5 Group readiness

Does every participant understand the meeting place and reservation period?

What exactly has the organiser secured?

How are cards, beverages, food choice and self-service coordinated?

How are changes in group size or needs handled?

4.6 Event readiness

Is the event free, registered, reserved or ticketed?

Where is the exact threshold?

Does event timing differ from ordinary venue hours?

Who owns changes, cancellations and entry recovery?

4.7 Arrival handoff

What should the guest be able to recognise immediately?

Which information should transfer into physical signage or staff awareness?

How should a group or access requirement be recognised without repetition?

Where does digital preparation end and live hospitality begin?

7. Pre-arrival readiness model

The strongest provisional model contains eight readiness layers.

7.1 Intent readiness

The visitor knows whether the visit is for:

casual food,

a specific vendor,

drinks,

a group gathering,

a free event,

a ticketed event,

family activity,

creative exploration,

or nightlife.

7.2 State readiness

The visitor has checked the relevant:

season,

date,

operating hours,

event state,

and high-consequence changes.

7.3 Route readiness

The visitor has a viable outward and return transport plan.

7.4 Weather readiness

The visitor understands expected conditions and the limits of covered alternatives.

7.5 Entitlement readiness

The visitor or organiser knows what is secured and what remains unreserved.

7.6 Personal readiness

Relevant mobility, dietary, sensory, family and care needs have an appropriate plan.

7.7 Party readiness

The members of a group have a consistent brief, meeting point and payment plan.

7.8 Handoff readiness

The guest knows what to recognise, where to go and who can help at arrival.

10. Same-day state check

The same-day check should answer:

What are today's relevant hours?

Is the intended event proceeding?

Is the relevant group arrangement unchanged?

Are there known access, transport or weather disruptions?

Does the intended vendor appear current?

Which uncertainties can only be resolved at the venue?

11. Operational source hierarchy

The strongest provisional source order is:

Tier 1 — Live service status

Current closure, disruption or direct operator communication.

Tier 2 — Dedicated current-state page

The official opening-hours and seasons page for ordinary venue clocks.

Tier 3 — Route-specific confirmation

A group confirmation, ticket or event-specific notice.

Tier 4 — General practical guidance

Evergreen directions, facilities and common rules.

Tier 5 — Editorial or archived content

Stories and older activity pages that demonstrate what has occurred but should not define current operations.

The hierarchy is an architecture inference.

Reffen does not publicly label these tiers.

13. Bicycle preparation

Reffen describes cycling along the waterfront as the most popular and likely easiest route.

It instructs visitors to park bicycles outside the venue, where space is described as available.

The pre-arrival questions include:

route conditions,

weather,

lighting for the return journey,

bicycle parking,

group cycling confidence,

child transport,

and alcohol-related return decisions.

The phrase “easiest” is a general institutional description.

It is not universally true for guests with mobility needs, children or no bicycle access.

14. Public-bus preparation

The official page states that bus 2A runs to and from Refshaleøen via Copenhagen Central Station approximately every ten minutes.

It identifies the Refshaleøen stop as a short walk from Reffen.

This is useful orientation.

The schedule remains controlled by the transport operator and may change.

Pre-arrival should therefore separate:

stable route identity,

current schedule,

live disruption,

stop accessibility,

and final walking segment.

Route Published by Venue

≠

Schedule Guaranteed by Venue

15. Harbour-bus preparation

The official page identifies harbour buses 991 and 992 and describes an approximate twenty-minute frequency.

It states that the Refshaleøen stop is a few minutes' walk from Reffen.

This route contributes to the waterfront experience.

It remains an external transport service.

The visitor may need to confirm:

current schedule,

service direction,

accessibility,

capacity,

weather disruption,

ticketing,

and return service after the intended visit.

The experiential value of travelling by water should not replace operational preparation.

16. Walking preparation

The official page describes the walk from Nyhavn as approximately 25–30 minutes.

This can make Reffen appear close and approachable.

The approximation does not account for:

exact starting point,

pace,

route familiarity,

mobility,

weather,

crowd conditions,

construction,

or the return journey.

The useful pre-arrival unit is not only minutes.

It is route fit.

F01 — Same-day hours test

Hypothesis: visitors can identify the correct current hours.

Falsifier: conflicting official surfaces produce different visit decisions.

F02 — Walk-in readiness test

Hypothesis: a first-time walk-in visitor can prepare without contacting the venue.

Falsifier: essential route or access information remains unavailable.

F03 — Bicycle-route test

Hypothesis: published bicycle guidance supports safe, appropriate preparation.

Falsifier: guests lack necessary route, parking or return information.

F04 — Bus-source test

Hypothesis: venue guidance successfully hands off to current operator schedules.

Falsifier: static venue information is mistaken for live service proof.

F05 — Harbour-bus test

Hypothesis: guests can confirm direction, ticketing, accessibility and return service.

Falsifier: the route remains experiential imagery rather than actionable transport.

F06 — Parking-boundary test

Hypothesis: drivers understand that nearby parking is externally operated.

Falsifier: payment or access failures are incorrectly attributed to Reffen.

F07 — Return-route test

Hypothesis: event and nightlife visitors consider departure before arrival.

Falsifier: arrival guidance creates no safe or viable return plan.

F08 — Weather-state test

Hypothesis: forecast information can be translated into usable preparation.

Falsifier: guests know the weather but not its effect on venue participation.

F09 — Covered-capacity test

Hypothesis: guests understand that shelter exists without assuming guaranteed space.

Falsifier: “covered seating” is interpreted as a reservation or capacity guarantee.

F10 — Crowd-readiness test

Hypothesis: the busy-day warning changes preparation for relevant visitors.

Falsifier: it remains too generic to support timing, mobility or family decisions.

F11 — Group brief test

Hypothesis: every participant can reach the correct area with the necessary expectations.

Falsifier: knowledge remains concentrated in the organiser.

F12 — Group-size-change test

Hypothesis: an updated party size produces capacity review and reconfirmation.

Falsifier: the venue discovers the mismatch only at arrival.

F13 — Reffen-card readiness test

Hypothesis: group cards can be distributed and used without confusion about value or entitlement.

Falsifier: users interpret value as a guaranteed product bundle or cannot recover failure.

F14 — Event-threshold test

Hypothesis: event visitors know where and when the specific entitlement is checked.

Falsifier: guests arrive at the general venue but cannot find or enter the event.

F15 — Dietary-route test

Hypothesis: the visitor can move from directory possibility to accountable vendor confirmation.

Falsifier: no reliable confirmation is available before purchase.

F16 — Outside-food exception test

Hypothesis: consequential medical or access needs have a dignified exception route.

Falsifier: the general rule prevents safe participation without review.

F17 — Accessible-route test

Hypothesis: public information can support an end-to-end plan.

Falsifier: facilities are described but the complete route cannot be established.

F18 — Sensory-readiness test

Hypothesis: a visitor can identify likely noise, crowd and stimulation conditions.

Falsifier: relevant sensory information is absent or purely promotional.

F19 — Family-readiness test

Hypothesis: families can distinguish current activities from archived or seasonal content.

Falsifier: visible activity pages are mistaken for live availability.

F20 — Requirement-acknowledgement test

Hypothesis: a disclosed need reaches an explicit accepted or declined state.

Falsifier: the guest arrives with only an unanswered message.

F21 — Multi-party privacy test

Falsifier: sensitive requirements are broadcast to the group.

F22 — Offline-access test

Falsifier: the guest loses the route, contact or entitlement when connectivity fails.

Falsifier: the guest must restart the booking or requirement explanation.
