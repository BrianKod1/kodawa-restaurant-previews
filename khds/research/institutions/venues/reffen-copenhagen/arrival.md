1. Purpose of this file

This file studies the transition from a planned visit to usable participation at Reffen.

Arrival is not treated as:

reaching Refshaleøen,

leaving a bus or harbour bus,

parking nearby,

seeing the container structures,

crossing an unstaffed threshold,

or joining the first visible queue.

Those may be arrival events.

None is sufficient on its own.

For this case, arrival is the live hospitality system that must convert:

a route-dependent approach,

a current venue state,

a general or specific guest intent,

an open or reserved participation route,

an unfamiliar multi-operator layout,

changing weather and crowd conditions,

and any relevant personal requirement

into a visitor who knows:

whether they are in the right place,

whether their intended route is operating,

where to go next,

what remains unreserved,

where essential facilities are,

who can help,

and how to recover if the expected route fails.

Reffen does not have a universal reception desk or check-in requirement for ordinary entry in the public evidence reviewed here.

This makes arrival less formal but not less consequential.

The purpose of this file is to identify:

where live hospitality begins,

how the threshold changes by transport and intent,

what orientation is required before self-directed exploration,

how group and event entitlements are recognised,

how weather, crowd and accessibility affect the arrival state,

where the first recoverable responsibility point should exist,

and what public evidence cannot establish about live performance.

2. Arrival boundary

Arrival begins when the visitor encounters the live place and must translate preparation into action.

Depending on the route, that can begin:

at the Refshaleøen bus stop,

at the harbour-bus stop,

at bicycle parking,

at a nearby car park,

at a mooring point,

on the walking approach,

at a visible venue entrance,

or at the first live event, group or vendor handoff.

Arrival ends when the guest reaches a settled participation state.

That state is not the same for every visitor.

2.4 Working definition

Arrival is the route-dependent live period in which a visitor recognises the venue, reconciles current conditions, receives sufficient orientation, finds the relevant participation object and reaches a usable or recoverable starting state.

3. Primary research question

How should an open-entry, multi-operator venue orient and receive visitors when the physical site is shared but each guest may be arriving for a different vendor, group area, event, activity or social purpose?

4. Secondary research questions

4.1 Recognition

Can visitors recognise Reffen from every published transport route?

Is the entrance or venue boundary visually unambiguous?

Can a first-time visitor distinguish Reffen from neighbouring Refshaleøen environments?

Does the container-city identity help recognition or increase wayfinding complexity?

4.2 Orientation

What must be understood at the threshold?

Can visitors locate food, bars, toilets, shelter, seating, events and assistance?

How much information can be presented without overwhelming a casual visitor?

Can orientation support exploration rather than over-control it?

4.3 Intent routing

How does a casual diner enter differently from a booked group or event attendee?

Can a visitor with no chosen vendor begin confidently?

Does the system distinguish venue-level and operator-level destinations?

Can a guest change intent after arrival?

4.4 Live conditions

How are closure, crowd, queue, weather and capacity changes made visible?

Does the physical venue reconcile conflicts in online information?

What happens when the preferred route is unavailable?

Can live uncertainty remain understandable without becoming chaotic?

4.5 Accessibility

Do published ramps and surfaces form an end-to-end usable route?

Can a guest reach accessible toilets without unnecessary barriers?

How do busy-day bottlenecks affect mobility and pram use?

Is human assistance identifiable before the guest becomes stranded?

4.6 Responsibility

Where is the first venue-owned help point?

Who receives a group, event or vendor problem?

Can venue staff route a problem without denying ownership of the initial response?

How does the guest learn where responsibility changes?

4.7 Settlement

When has an ordinary walk-in visitor completed arrival?

When has a group completed arrival?

When has an event attendee completed arrival?

Can the guest proceed without repeating pre-arrival information?

8. The moving arrival threshold

Reffen's arrival threshold changes with the route and guest intent.

8.1 Bicycle visitor

The threshold may begin when the guest leaves the waterfront route, identifies bicycle parking and transitions to walking.

8.2 Bus visitor

The threshold may begin at the Refshaleøen stop, where the guest must identify the correct final walking direction.

8.3 Harbour-bus visitor

The threshold may begin at the landing, where the guest transitions from water transport to the site approach.

8.4 Walking visitor

The threshold may unfold gradually as industrial surroundings become the venue environment.

8.5 Car visitor

The threshold may begin at externally operated parking before the guest reaches Reffen.

8.6 Private-boat visitor

The threshold may begin through an externally operated mooring arrangement.

8.7 Group visitor

The threshold may be the point where the organiser, area or reservation is recognised.

8.8 Event visitor

The threshold may be a sub-venue entry or ticket checkpoint inside the wider free-entry site.

8.9 Design consequence

Arrival guidance should not assume that every guest approaches from the same direction or seeks the same first object.

9. Arrival layer model

Layer 1 — External route completion

The guest leaves bicycle, bus, harbour bus, walking, car or boat transport.

Layer 2 — Directional recognition

The guest identifies the path toward Reffen.

Layer 3 — Venue recognition

The guest recognises the public venue boundary.

Layer 4 — Current-state reconciliation

The guest learns whether the expected venue, event, area or vendor state remains current.

Layer 5 — Essential orientation

The guest identifies:

food,

event spaces,

The guest moves toward the relevant:

event threshold,

activity,

Layer 7 — Entitlement recognition

Where required, a booking, ticket, group name or other participation object is recognised.

10. Route-to-venue handoff

The final walking segment is common to several routes.

Bus and harbour-bus visitors are described as arriving a few minutes' walk from Reffen.

Drivers and private-boat visitors also leave externally operated infrastructure before reaching the venue.

The route-to-venue handoff should answer:

Am I walking in the correct direction?

What landmark identifies the venue?

Which entrance suits my intent or access need?

Is the expected operating state still current?

Where does Reffen's responsibility begin?

The public evidence establishes the route descriptions.

It does not establish the quality of the final physical wayfinding.

11. Bicycle arrival

Bicycle arrival contains three distinct objects:

navigation to Refshaleøen,

The guest needs to understand:

where cycling ends,

where the bicycle can be left,

whether the parking is within view,

which path becomes pedestrian,

The published instruction to park outside the venue creates a threshold before venue entry.

36. Waiting taxonomy at arrival

Reffen can expose all five canonical waiting types.

36.1 Access waiting

Waiting to enter an event, area or constrained threshold.

36.2 Resource waiting

Waiting for a table, covered area, toilet key or shared facility.

36.3 Product waiting

Waiting for an ordered item to be prepared.

36.4 Service waiting

Waiting to order, pay or receive assistance.

36.5 Exception waiting

Waiting for dietary clarification, group correction, payment recovery or access support.

Several waits can overlap.

The arrival system should distinguish them because each has a different owner and recovery route.

The guest needs a visible first point that can:

receive the question,

answer or route it,

38. First owned handoff

The first owned handoff is the first point at which Reffen or the relevant operator knowingly receives the arriving visitor's state.

Examples may include:

a group host recognising the organiser,

39. Recognition versus verification

Group and event arrivals may require verification.

The system should distinguish:

recognising the person or party,

verifying the entitlement,

reconciling changed details,

and orienting the guest.

Recognition can reduce friction.

Verification can protect capacity and fairness.

Neither should expose unnecessary personal information.

41. Arrival-state reconciliation

The receiving layer should compare:

Expected State

with

Live State

Possible outcomes are:

Match

The expected route proceeds.

Minor variance

The route remains usable with an explanation.

Material variance with alternative

The intended route fails, but another appropriate route exists.

Material variance without immediate alternative

The guest requires recovery, waiting or departure support.

The visitor should not have to infer which outcome applies.

42. Arrival voice

Arrival language should be:

directional,

current,

specific,

calm,

inclusive,

and ownership-aware.

Useful examples of function include:

identify where the visitor is,

state what is open,

point to the next relevant route,

explain a change,

and name who can help.

Weak arrival language relies only on:

“good vibes,”

“something for everyone,”

“plenty of space,”

or similar atmosphere promises.

Those phrases may support tone.

They do not resolve live participation.

43. Settled-state test

A general visitor is settled when they:

recognise the venue,

understand the current state,

know where essential facilities and help are,

and can begin choosing or exploring.

A group visitor is settled when:

the area is recognised,

the entitlement is reconciled,

participants can locate it,

and payment or self-service instructions are usable.

An event visitor is settled when:

the correct threshold is found,

the access object is accepted or clearly resolved,

and the participant can enter the event state.

A visitor with an access requirement is settled when:

the usable route and assistance are available,

not merely when the person reaches the site.

49. Provisional arrival findings

49.1 Open-entry venues still have arrival systems

Removing formal check-in increases the importance of environmental orientation and visible help.

49.2 Arrival is route-dependent

Bicycle, bus, harbour bus, walking, car and boat routes create different final handoffs.

49.3 Arrival is intent-dependent

A general visitor, group participant and event attendee can cross the same venue boundary while remaining in different arrival states.

49.4 Architecture can support recognition and still burden wayfinding

The container city creates visual distinction but may also produce competing cues.

49.5 Live state can invalidate a successful pre-arrival plan

Vendor, event, seating, weather and crowd changes must be reconciled physically.

49.6 Seating can be arrival infrastructure

Finding an appropriate shared base may precede ordering for groups, families and access-sensitive visitors.

49.7 Human assistance is an exception interface

The venue needs visible reception capacity even when ordinary participation remains self-directed.

49.8 Responsibility can begin with routing

Reffen may not own an external or vendor failure, but it can own the first useful direction inside its shared environment.

49.9 Settlement is route-specific

Arrival completes when the relevant participation can begin, not merely when the guest enters.

49.10 Institution transfer remains unvalidated

This one venue case does not establish recurrence or canonical maturity movement.

51. Falsification programme

F01 — Route recognition test

Hypothesis: visitors from each published transport route can identify Reffen without assistance.

Falsifier: repeated wrong turns or reliance on informal directions.

F02 — Entrance recognition test

Hypothesis: first-time visitors can distinguish the venue boundary.

Falsifier: the industrial context makes the entrance ambiguous.

F03 — Map transfer test

Hypothesis: the published map supports physical orientation.

Falsifier: labels, scale or landmark relationships do not match first-use needs.

F04 — Threshold information test

Hypothesis: visitors can identify intent, essentials, changes and help without overload.

Falsifier: optional promotional content obscures immediate action.

F05 — Walk-in settlement test

Hypothesis: a visitor with no reservation can begin confidently.

Falsifier: first-time participation depends on following other guests or asking vendors unrelated questions.

F06 — Group recognition test

Hypothesis: organiser and participants can locate the reserved area.

Falsifier: only the organiser can navigate or staff cannot find the record.

F07 — Group time test

Hypothesis: the two-hour entitlement begins at a clearly understood point.

Falsifier: late or staggered arrival creates disputed duration.

F08 — Event-threshold test

Hypothesis: event participants distinguish general entry from event access.

Falsifier: guests reach Reffen but cannot identify the correct queue or sub-venue.

F09 — Vendor-state test

Hypothesis: visitors can determine whether a preferred vendor is currently usable.

Falsifier: the guest must physically search several stalls or receives stale information.

F10 — Alternative-route test

Hypothesis: a closed vendor does not collapse the whole visit.

Falsifier: relevant alternatives are not discoverable.

F11 — Seating-before-order test

Hypothesis: guests who need a base can identify suitable seating before purchasing.

Falsifier: ordering creates a product without a usable place to consume it.

F12 — Weather-degraded test

Hypothesis: arrival remains understandable when guests concentrate in covered areas.

Falsifier: shelter demand obscures routes, queues or assistance.

F13 — Crowd bottleneck test

Hypothesis: accessible and pram routes remain usable or recoverable during peak conditions.

Falsifier: published access features become operationally unavailable.

F14 — Toilet-key test

Hypothesis: a visitor can locate and obtain the required key without excessive search.

Falsifier: urgency is increased by unclear location or unavailable key holders.

F15 — Human-help test

Hypothesis: the first staff member can answer or route an arrival question.

Falsifier: the guest is repeatedly redirected without context transfer.

F16 — No-reset test

Hypothesis: prepared group, event and access information remains usable at arrival.

Falsifier: the guest must repeat or prove previously accepted details without reason.

F17 — Recognition/privacy test

Hypothesis: group or event recognition can occur without exposing unnecessary information.

Falsifier: names, access needs or payment states are publicly disclosed.

F18 — Live-state reconciliation test

Hypothesis: physical information resolves meaningful differences from online expectations.

Falsifier: guests discover changes only after joining a queue or reaching a closed area.

F19 — External-boundary test

Hypothesis: parking or mooring problems can be routed to the correct operator.

Falsifier: shared destination language creates responsibility confusion.

F20 — Settled-state test

Hypothesis: arrival completion predicts the ability to participate.

Falsifier: visitors classified as arrived still lack essential orientation or access.

F21 — Low-vision and non-visual wayfinding test

Hypothesis: arrival does not depend entirely on visual container recognition.

Falsifier: non-visual and low-vision visitors cannot identify route, threshold or help.

F22 — Language test

Hypothesis: essential arrival information remains understandable to relevant international visitors.

Falsifier: physical directions or changes are available only through inaccessible language or staff assumptions.

F23 — Emergency-orientation test

Hypothesis: visitors can identify urgent help and exit routes.

Falsifier: normal exploratory wayfinding provides no emergency legibility.

F24 — Transfer-validity test

Hypothesis: Layered Arrival remains useful when arrival has no formal check-in.

Falsifier: the pattern requires a hosted reception sequence to remain explanatory.
