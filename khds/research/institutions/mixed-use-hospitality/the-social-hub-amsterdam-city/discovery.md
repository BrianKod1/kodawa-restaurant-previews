# Discovery

## Research question

How does a user understand a property that is simultaneously a hotel, student residence, coworking environment, meeting/event venue, restaurant/bar and community hub?

## Evidence

The Amsterdam City page presents several participation routes: hotel stay, student accommodation, coworking, meetings/events, food and community activity. [S1]

The 2022 rebrand from The Student Hotel to The Social Hub was explicitly framed as a move toward a broader hybrid-hospitality identity connecting students, travelers, entrepreneurs and local communities. [S9]

The coworking factsheet separately presents flexible workspace, dedicated desks and private offices with different benefits and access conditions. [S3]

The meetings/events factsheet presents another commercial route with dedicated event spaces, hosts, catering and hotel-linked benefits. [S5]

## Interpretation

The discovery problem is not simply choosing a room. A user must first identify which relationship fits their need.

```text
One Property
!=
One Customer Journey
```

The property can be discovered as lodging, housing, workspace, event infrastructure, dining or community space.

That creates a useful KHDS distinction:

```text
Brand-Level Discoverability
!=
Product-Level Comprehension
```

A brand can successfully communicate breadth while an individual user may still need to understand eligibility, duration, access rights, pricing model and capacity for a specific product.

## Limits

Public pages show the offered pathways but do not establish whether users understand the distinctions correctly, which channels generate the most qualified demand, or how often people move from one product relationship into another.

## Synthesis-gate relevance

Case #30 tests whether institutional complexity begins before booking. The evidence supports multiple discovery routes under one brand, but not a single unified funnel.
