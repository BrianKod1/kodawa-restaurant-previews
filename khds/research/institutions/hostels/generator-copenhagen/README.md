## Institution

Generator Copenhagen is a centrally located accommodation and social-hospitality institution offering:

* private rooms;
* family rooms;
* private multi-bed rooms;
* mixed shared dormitories;
* women-only shared dormitories;
* individual beds within shared rooms;
* group accommodation;
* a bar and restaurant;
* event space;
* luggage storage;
* laundry;
* bicycle hire;
* games and social areas;
* a pétanque bar and terrace;
* and 24-hour reception.

The institution describes itself as a design-led hostel and presents the stay as more than accommodation alone.

Its public proposition combines:

* sleep;
* city access;
* social interaction;
* dining;
* games;
* events;
* and group participation.

The property therefore creates multiple overlapping guest states:

* individual traveller;
* private-room guest;
* shared-dormitory guest;
* women-only dormitory guest;
* family guest;
* group traveller;
* event participant;
* bar or restaurant customer;
* game participant;
* and city visitor using the property as an orientation point.

These states may share one institutional environment without sharing the same:

* privacy conditions;
* booking object;
* eligibility rules;
* social expectations;
* safety requirements;
* noise tolerance;
* financial commitment;
* or recovery route.

## Why Case #25

Before Case #25, KHDS had studied:

* a destination hotel;
* a wellness institution;
* an open-air multi-operator venue;
* and an indoor multi-operator market.

Case #25 introduced the first hostel and social-stay institution.

A hostel is not simply a less expensive hotel.

Its hospitality model may depend on coordinating:

* private and shared sleeping arrangements;
* strangers occupying the same room;
* individual bed inventory inside room-level capacity;
* optional social participation;
* personal security;
* quiet and active zones;
* age and guardian rules;
* group and individual bookings;
* shared bathrooms or en-suite facilities;
* luggage storage;
* late-night return;
* and institutional responsibility across an overnight period.

Generator Copenhagen was selected because its public system combines accommodation with dining, events, games and social spaces while retaining materially different room and bed configurations.

That structure creates a direct pressure test for:

## Primary Research Question

> How should hospitality preserve privacy, safety, agency and reliable recovery when sleeping space, social space and institutional responsibility are shared?

---

## Supporting Questions

Case #25 examined:

1. How discovery distinguishes a social-stay proposition from a conventional hotel stay.

2. Whether the booking system clearly distinguishes:

   * one bed;
   * one private room;
   * multiple beds;
   * a complete dormitory;
   * a family room;
   * and a group booking.

3. What a guest must understand before committing to shared accommodation.

4. How age, identification, guardian and room-eligibility rules are communicated.

5. How pre-arrival information prepares guests for:

   * transport;
   * check-in;
   * luggage;
   * payment;
   * identification;
   * room sharing;
   * noise;
   * security;
   * and social participation.

6. How physical arrival separates:

   * reception;
   * accommodation access;
   * public social spaces;
   * dining;
   * games;
   * events;
   * and private sleeping areas.

7. Which forms of social participation are genuinely optional.

8. How quiet, privacy and rest are protected inside an institution that promotes social activity.

9. How responsibility changes when a guest books one bed inside a room occupied by strangers.

10. What published recovery routes exist for:

    * room-allocation problems;
    * incompatible shared-room conditions;
    * lost or insecure belongings;
    * accessibility failure;
    * noise;
    * harassment;
    * check-in failure;
    * and group separation.

11. Whether photography demonstrates the spatial and social differences among private rooms, shared dormitories and public areas.

12. Whether the institutional story explains the operating model or allows lifestyle language to conceal important participation conditions.

## Falsification Targets Carried Forward

Institution comparison and future cases should determine whether:

1. a bed-level booking gives the guest sufficient pre-commitment knowledge of the actual shared-room condition;

2. social positioning preserves meaningful permission not to participate;

3. private, shared, family, women-only and group accommodation routes remain operationally distinct;

4. 24-hour reception functions as meaningful recovery infrastructure rather than only published availability;

5. house rules create enforceable protection for rest and personal boundaries;

6. bed-level storage and privacy features meaningfully support shared-room use;

7. guests with accessibility requirements can verify an appropriate room before financial commitment;

8. room-allocation failure has a recoverable route;

9. nightlife and event activity remain compatible with overnight care;

10. the institution can preserve hospitality responsibility when a guest’s most consequential conditions are created partly by other guests;

11. shared-condition legibility transfers to other hostel and social-stay models;

12. optional activities can be governed when their consequences reach non-participants;

13. public social access remains distinct from protected resident access;

14. and guest participation can remain optional without weakening the institutional story.
